# The Valcárcel Estate Library — Archive of the First Crossing

## Status: established regional canon
The **main Valcárcel family line has continuously retained custody of the original founding-voyage logbooks and the four surviving portraits of Alonso, Sáweli/Inés, their wedding and their nine children**. These treasures have **not** passed into a public museum collection or disappeared into unconnected private collections. They have remained family heirlooms within the **private library and archive of the principal Valcárcel estate** from the colonial generations to the contemporary era.

The physical library has not necessarily occupied the same room or building for four centuries. The archive may have been moved within family properties, sheltered during disasters, and conserved or rebound. **Continuous family custody is the claim, not 418 years of untouched storage.**

## An enduring private institution
The estate's library began as the documentary heart of Alonso and Sáweli's household: correspondence, the 1608 registers, legal and merchant papers, charts, devotional books, family records, and the images painted by Alonso's expedition companion and friend, the celebrated Spanish artist provisionally called **Mateo de Villacorta**.

As the family prospered, successive generations expanded the holdings. Its reading and picture rooms became a dynastic institution rather than a mere cabinet of curiosities. The archival paintings hung in protected rooms or were occasionally brought out for family commemorations; some were removed for professional conservation, but ownership and custody remained in the principal family line.

The setting has not yet settled whether the present library occupies a preserved colonial wing, a later eighteenth-century residence, or a nineteenth-century expanded estate complex. A plausible solution is **a seventeenth-century nucleus incorporated into a larger, later house**, with heavily modernized fire, humidity, seismic, and security protection. The estate's exact location is also open, though it should be near enough to Santa Aurelia's historic core to participate in the city's civic memory.

## The four paintings
All are **original seventeenth-century artworks, not later commemorative re-creations**, with period paintings and subsequent conservation work distinguished:
1. **The Arrival of Don Alonso de Valcárcel y Austria**, c.1608–1610 (painting date provisional).
2. **Portrait of Sáweli / Doña Inés**, later 1610s–1620s (date provisional).
3. **The Marriage of Alonso and Sáweli / Inés**, c.1611–1613 (date provisional).
4. **The Valcárcel Family at Santa Aurelia**, c.1633–1636 (date provisional), showing Alonso, Sáweli, and **all nine children**.

These works are recorded in the family's own painting catalogue and their movement/conservation records. The same painter was a **personal friend of Alonso who traveled with the 1608 founding colony**. The painter's exact working name remains provisional.

## The founding logbooks and original passenger rolls
The **1608 *Registro del Viaje del Norte*** and associated ship manifest, complementary folios from the smaller tender, cargo accounts, and later annotations also belong to the family archive. The original manuscript records **67 founders and voyage personnel on two vessels** in the current working reconstruction. See [the entire historical manuscript](16-founding-fleet-logbook-1608.md).

Some shipboard originals are fragile, stained, incomplete or rebound. Scholars may rely on authenticated family transcripts, facsimiles, and subsequent collations; those derivative copies do not replace the privately held originals.

The family may retain both Alonso's personal papers and records prepared by the expedition's notary. How each entered the family's legal ownership should be explained in future property and archival histories. The archive also contains material referencing Indigenous communities, and descendants' custodianship does **not** extinguish Indigenous cultural rights or grant the family unilateral authority to interpret or publish culturally restricted knowledge.

## The Cascadia earthquake, 1700
The archive's preservation through the January 1700 catastrophe is an essential episode in its story. At this point its exact escape remains to be dramatized. A likely historical account has an heir ordering trunks of paintings, bindings, and registers moved to elevated estate storage before or after the disaster's first shocks, with some records subsequently recovered from damaged family properties. **The story must preserve the survival of the original objects without pretending that preservation was effortless.**

## Family, scholars, and public interest
The family collection is **private property**, even though its artifacts are of immense public significance. Access can be granted by invitation, research agreement, controlled supervised viewing, loan, digitized exhibit, or authorized facsimile. The Santa Aurelia maritime museum, Grand Willowwick Library, university historians, conservators, and the Ashalewan and other relevant Indigenous cultural authorities might collaborate with the family on particular works. This does **not** mean any originals have been permanently donated.

A family-held archive can be respected and valuable while remaining contested: scholars may challenge transcriptions, dates or selectively curated materials; some communities seek access to records concerning their ancestors. A living family institution has obligations, choices and occasional disputes, not effortless control over the entire story.

## Expanded collection of manuscripts and historical artifacts
The estate's original holdings extend well beyond the four paintings and fleet registers. **Established by the user:** love letters between Alonso and Sáweli/Inés; original journals of numerous founding settlers; **Sáweli/Inés's own personal diary notebooks**, including detailed retrospective accounts of her **Ashalewan life before Alonso's arrival**, written **after** she learned alphabetic reading and writing in adult life; and extensive artifacts associated with the early settlement and household. These manuscripts are held with the original paintings and ship logbooks by the **principal Valcárcel line**.

The detailed, numbered scholarly inventory—correspondence, her retrospective diary, other settlers' journals, household registers, charts, heirlooms, textiles, navigational instruments, painter's tools and conservation records—is now contained in [the Estate Library Collection Catalogue](18-estate-library-collection-catalogue.md). Its sample translated quotations are **authored in-world source excerpts** rather than real-world archival discoveries. The library holds originals and identifies contemporary autographs, dictated records, later copies, and translations separately.

## The Sáweli Historical Corpus
The estate holds an exceptional body of original texts authored or compiled by **Sáweli/Inés**, including extensive **first-person later-life recollections of pre-1608 Ashalewan life**, travel notes, family registers and **interviews with other Indigenous peoples**. She compiled especially influential early written testimony on the Blackwood stoneways, prehistoric circle, White Ones, woodland lights, wolves and local legends. These original early-seventeenth-century witness accounts—not only later folklore and oral traditions—form a foundation for historical inquiry. The family's private custody of the folios does not entitle it to speak for the descendant communities. See [Sáweli/Inés as Historian](19-saweli-ines-historian-and-oral-histories.md).
