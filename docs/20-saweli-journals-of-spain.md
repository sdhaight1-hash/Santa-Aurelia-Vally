# Sáweli / Doña Inés — Journals of the Journeys to Spain

*In-world archival guide, newly developed for the alternate-history Santa Aurelia setting. Names, voyage dates, diary extracts and incidents below are invented historical material within the setting.*

## Established facts

**Sáweli (Doña Inés de Santa Aurelia) accompanied her husband, Don Alonso de Valcárcel y Austria, on visits back to Spain from time to time and kept extensive journals of those journeys.** Her writing illuminates Spanish social and political life in the early seventeenth century through the perspective of an educated Indigenous woman from the Columbia estuary, herself an accomplished historian of Ashalewan and neighboring peoples. The voyages extend rather than replace her deep engagement with the oral histories and traditions of her homeland.

These are **her own authored travel notebooks**, preserved with the *Sáweli Historical Corpus*, love letters, four founding portraits, and 1608 fleet records in the **private Valcárcel Estate Library**, continuously held by the main family line.

## A plausible itinerary and chronology

Santa Aurelia is on the northwestern American Pacific coast, and **there is no quick direct coastal voyage to Spain**. A likely outward route proceeds south by sea to Acapulco or another New Spanish harbor; then overland across central New Spain to Veracruz, followed by an Atlantic passage to the Iberian Peninsula. Other legs could require waiting for convoy schedules, weather, commercial obligations or politically authorized passage. The return retraces connections in reverse. Exact logistics, month-by-month ship names and travel costs remain working details.

The following **two major sojourns are proposed**, not yet strictly dated canon:

**First Iberian journey, c.1624–1625:** After the birth of Tomás in 1623 and before Leonor in 1626, Sáweli visits the Valcárcel and Rojas extended families with Alonso. She is in her early thirties, and already capable of substantial independent writing. The couple's six children, all young, are cared for by a wider household and extended Indigenous kin network during their absence, if we keep this itinerary. These arrangements and the emotional cost should be documented rather than assumed effortless.

**Second Iberian journey, c.1635–1637:** After the birth of the ninth child in 1632 and after the proposed early-1630s family portrait, Sáweli and Alonso visit Spain again on business involving the port, inheritance, credit and the expanding colonial household. Sáweli is now an established historian with years of interviews and writing. Family members at home, including older daughters and sons, help organize the household; the youngest child is still very young, so the travel needs a credible caregiving arrangement. This later visit can frame the return of manuscripts, gifts and paintings to the estate.

Both travels were **long, exhausting intercontinental enterprises**. The setting can add an earlier trip only if it is reconciled with childbirth, the speed and safety of early-seventeenth-century travel, and other established history.

## The Iberian Journals — proposed catalogue series

**VA-SAW-009 — *Diario del Primer Regreso* (c.1624–1625).** Sáweli's first substantial Iberian travel notebook. Describes the New Spanish crossing and her first experience of old European urban life: harbors, narrow streets, stone buildings, merchants, parish processions, travelers from many continents, household customs, markets, unfamiliar food, courtly etiquette, languages and Alonso's relatives. The phrase *Primer Regreso* (“First Return”) was assigned by a later Spanish-speaking archivist and describes **Alonso's return**, not Sáweli returning to a country she had never previously visited.

**VA-SAW-010 — *Cuaderno de las Ciudades de España* (c.1624–1637).** Comparative studies of Cádiz/Seville, Madrid and smaller towns as visited. The notebooks contrast their architecture, markets, social stratification, family relationships, treatment of women, servants, migrants and religious minorities, poor relief and commercial institutions with the life Sáweli knew on the Columbia. Locations are a proposed route, not all proven destinations.

**VA-SAW-011 — *Conversaciones de las Casas y la Corte* (c.1624–1637).** Records of conversations in Spanish households and possibly court-connected circles, reflecting Alonso's offshoot Habsburg family status. Sáweli documents the fascination, exoticism and suspicion directed toward her, while also forming independent relationships with women, merchants, scholars, household workers and artists. **The exact court audiences and named historical participants remain undecided; no invented meetings with real monarchs are assumed fact.**

**VA-SAW-012 — *Libro del Segundo Viaje* (c.1635–1637).** Mature reflections from a later journey: reunions, old friendships, more fluent writing, political change in Spain, family responsibilities at home and renewed observations of Spanish society. Contrasts her younger impressions with a more experienced understanding. Her interest in the historical archives and genealogy of Alonso's family grows, but the notebooks do not treat Habsburg descent as giving either spouse a sovereign claim over Indigenous lands.

**VA-SAW-013 — *Cartas entre Dos Océanos* (c.1624–1637).** Letters home to her children, mother-side relatives and estate caretakers, plus received correspondence delayed by transoceanic travel. They reveal emotional and practical costs: news arriving late, worrying about children, household accounts, the possibility of shipwreck, and the difficulty of maintaining relationships across oceans. Some originals are in her hand; other letters are retained copies.

**VA-SAW-014 — *Apuntes para una Historia Comparada* (c.1630–1640).** Her comparative historical reflections: what Spaniards choose to write down, what Ashalewan historians transmit orally, the politics of naming and translation, historical bias, how coastal communities describe strangers and how assumptions about nobility or land ownership differ. A powerful companion to the Blackwood interview folios.

## The observer and her voice

Sáweli is not written as a passive court curiosity or an object of universal European adoration. Her position as Alonso's wife and a respected member of an established colonial household affords travel, introductions and certain protections, but she encounters unfamiliar prejudice, curiosity and expectations. Her writings preserve her judgments—sometimes admiring, sometimes critical—without assuming she instantly comprehends all European customs.

She sees impressive buildings and skilled artisans, yet misses the Columbia's waterways. She studies books and painters, but notices that official European chronicles are as selective about what they record as some colonial paperwork. Her own people and neighbors remain her point of reference, not a vanished stage of her life.

## Four newly authored in-world excerpts

> **On first seeing Spain, first journey (proposed c.1624):** “Alonso called this his country, though I saw him look at the harbor as if he were a stranger. There were so many ships and so many stones that I wondered where the earth had room to breathe. He smiled when I asked him, and said he had once wondered the same thing of our forests.”

> **On the women of a Spanish household (proposed c.1625):** “They asked me who rules the river people. I told them that they had asked a question too small for the answer. They then asked if my husband permitted me to write. I showed them the pages I had written and asked whether ink required a husband's permission to dry.”

> **On Spanish chronicles (proposed c.1636):** “I was shown a book in which a voyage began when a captain saw land. I asked whether the people upon that land were not already living in the years before his ship. The keeper of the book said it was the manner of histories to begin with the discoverer. I told him that this was the manner of some histories, not of the world.”

> **On leaving Spain a second time (proposed c.1637):** “I go home carrying books and questions. I leave behind friends and people who will never understand my river unless they have heard it in the rain. Alonso says he has spent his life between two shores. I answer that we have taught our children to see more than two.”

All excerpts are fiction from the invented in-world journal, not literal quotations from any real historic Indigenous person.

## Relation to her Blackwood and White Ones research

Her experience in Spain sharpens her method as an interviewer and historian. She finds old European tales of ancient stones, giants, ghost lights and wilderness beings sometimes superficially similar to Blackwood traditions, but **does not claim that likeness proves common ancestry, Druids, or historical travel from Britain to Oregon**. Comparisons grow more interesting because she has firsthand familiarity with cultural difference, translation and outsiders' fascination with mythology.

It is especially striking that her **Blackwood interviews were recorded in the 1620s–1630s** and survive independently of later nineteenth-century Scottish, Irish, Romanian and Slavic interpretations. Her Iberian journals do not displace the original Indigenous accounts; they furnish a second comparative perspective.

## Material preservation

The two voyage books and related notebooks are original items in the main Valcárcel family's private archive, with long-term restoration, page conservation and later translations. Some letters were stored in household bundles separate from sewn notebooks, and some folios may have been rearranged by later family archivists. The proposed catalogue IDs VA-SAW-009 through VA-SAW-014 continue the historical series begun with her estuary autobiographical works and Blackwood interview records.

**Canon boundary:** trips to Spain with Alonso and authored journals are **user established**. The two exact itineraries, years, contents, catalogue numbers and sample passages are **working lore** until refined.

## The courtly celebrations that inspired the hall
Sáweli delighted in the performances, music, dances, courtly receptions, elaborate dress, and gathering spaces she encountered in Spanish elite households and, in the working reconstruction, certain court-connected events. **Her fascination inspired Alonso to build the original Casa de las Asambleas as a gift for her**, completed provisionally in **1638** after the couple's Spanish journeys. She helped shape it as a social space for Santa Aurelia, not merely as an imitation of Spanish nobility. Her proposed later travel journals can preserve floor sketches, musical observations, conversation about the first great fête and her own ambitions for the hall. Such details are invented in-world documentation until written as specific folios. See [the hall](21-casa-de-las-asambleas-love-gift.md).
