# The Valcárcel Estate Library: Catalogue of the First Generations

*Invented archival catalogue for an alternate-history Santa Aurelia, recording original documents, objects, copies, and the limits of their interpretation.*

## Custody and the collection's purpose

The principal Valcárcel family has continuously held this collection, inherited from the founding household of **Alonso de Valcárcel y Austria** and **Sáweli (Doña Inés)**. The four original colonial portraits and the 1608 founding-fleet logbooks anchor it, but the library is a much larger archive spanning household life, maritime enterprise, early colonial administration, Ashalewan kinship, personal writings, devotional practice, commerce, medicine, trade, navigation, and preservation. **Continuity of the family collection does not imply that every object was physically present in 1608, or that the present building is original.**

The holdings below are **established as in-world collection categories**, with catalog identifiers, dates, authors and individual contents written as **working detail** for later development. They are genuine historical objects *within the setting*; their existence must not be confused with real archived manuscripts of Oregon.

## A. Founding voyage and naval records

**VA-MAR-001 — *Registro del Viaje del Norte* (1608).** Original bound logbook and muster of the flagship *Nuestra Señora de Santa Aurelia*, with Salcedo's dated annotations, ration disputes, observations of the estuary, wage claims and later repairs. Part of a two-vessel founding record with **67 named personnel** in the current reconstruction. See [complete working logbook](16-founding-fleet-logbook-1608.md).

**VA-MAR-002 — *Libro del Patache San Nicolás* (1608).** Tender manifest, separate accounts maintained by Tiburcio Reyes, instruments and cargo reconciliation. Its distinct hand sometimes contradicts the flagship's more favorable reports.

**VA-MAR-003 — Northern Soundings and River Bar Sketches (1608–1614).** Damp-damaged lead-line readings and painted coastal views, in the hands of chief pilot Baltasar de Urdaneta, Esteban Palacios and other sailors. Some charts were corrected after Indigenous pilots demonstrated safe passages. Colonial sketches are not proof that these waterways were previously unknown.

**VA-MAR-004 — The First Harbor Accounts (1608–1628).** Customs receipts, private freight, wage disputes, repairs, trade debts and shipping credit from New Spain, including separate earned-wage claims for Filipino and Indigenous New Spanish workers.

**VA-MAR-005 — The Captain's Letter Book (1606–1640).** Outgoing and incoming merchant correspondence, letters to Catalina and the Valcárcel/Rojas household, patrons and officials. Original letters survive alongside Alonso's retained drafts and clerical copies.

## B. Letters between Alonso and Sáweli / Inés

**VA-COR-001 — *Cartas de las Dos Orillas*, the Letters of the Two Shores (1609–1639).** A privately bundled collection of love letters, notes, borrowed books with messages, folded household papers, and letters transmitted when one spouse traveled. The correspondence follows their early affection, marriage, the arrival of nine children, and years of separation on trading voyages. Different materials and scripts identify distinct stages of their lives.

**Literacy chronology:** Inés's earliest communication to Alonso in 1609–1611 is preserved through messages dictated to a trusted writer, marked by personal symbols, annotated by an interpreter, or delivered orally and later transcribed. **She did not write fluent Spanish in 1608.** Starting around **1612–1615**, she progressively learned alphabetic reading and writing, initially in Spanish with help from Alonso, the colony's schoolmaster and women who could read. Her later original letters are increasingly in her own hand. This does **not** imply she was uneducated before contact: she possessed sophisticated Ashalewan oral, practical, navigational, genealogical and cultural knowledge.

The letter bundle contains a famous early dictated message, original short messages she wrote while learning, and mature letters in which she writes with ease, sometimes recording Ashalewan names and phrases phonetically. Scholars distinguish actual autograph leaves from scribal transcriptions.

**VA-COR-002 — The Courtship Packet (1609–1611).** Informal tokens and messages preserved separately from the larger bundle, including a draft of Alonso's marriage declaration. A beautiful public legend says his first written declaration was folded inside the wrapper of a gift of drawing paper, but the actual object remains open to examination.

**VA-COR-003 — Letters from the Inland Journeys (1618–1634).** During exploratory and commercial travel Alonso wrote to his wife about weather, river levels, negotiations and missing his family. Her replies could include instructions on estate management and the health of their growing children. These documents provide evidence of a working partnership, not solely romantic sentiment.

### Three invented excerpts for the collection

> **Alonso to Sáweli, early courtship, c.1610 (translation):** “The sea taught me to look for harbors. I thought that meant finding a place to anchor. I did not understand until I met you that a harbor can be a person.”

> **Sáweli to Alonso, dictated c.1611 (recorded by an intermediary):** “You speak of the ships as if they brought the whole world to our shore. I tell you that the river knew a thousand names before your sails appeared. Come listen, and I will teach you one of them.”

> **Sáweli to Alonso, in her own hand, c.1626 (translated):** “I miss you, but I will not write only of longing. The winter stores are counted; Juan Alonso has learned to argue with his uncle; and Catalina believes the little ones obey her. They do not. Return before the pear tree flowers again.”

All quotations are **original in-world historical prose** and not quotations from any genuine archival source.

## C. Sáweli / Inés's personal writings

**VA-DIA-001 — *Cuadernos de Sáweli*, the Sáweli Notebooks (c.1616–1636).** The collection's most extraordinary personal source: several small handwritten volumes compiled **after Inés learned to read and write as an adult**. The earlier sections are **retrospective recollections** of her life before Alonso and before the colony's founding, not implausible contemporaneous childhood diaries.

She recounts childhood in the Ashalewan estuary community: seasonal household movements, the work of river and sea, family instruction and expectations, the temperaments of her siblings **Kévarin, Anéwi and Tórewan**, the influence of her mother **Elámi** and grandmothers **Meyála and Olávi**, kinship obligations, food preservation, winter gatherings, the interpretation of weather, community disputes, visits to relatives, and her own changing understanding of political leadership. She also gives an indigenous perspective on the arrivals of ships and the adjustments and conflicts that followed. Her recollections contain anecdotes, humor, grief, disagreements with her husband and corrections of the Spanish notary's descriptions.

Some passages may describe the Blacksilver Willow or old stoneways from the family's travel traditions, but these should not imply Sáweli's nation is identical to every Blackwood-associated Indigenous nation. The diaries may include private knowledge that the family archivists and relevant Indigenous cultural authorities do not make public.

**VA-DIA-002 — The Childhood Recollections (first composed c.1617–1622).** A titled subset of the notebooks, covering childhood and young adulthood before 1608: learning canoe travel, gathering and preservation, family gatherings and a first independent dispute about a river crossing. She acknowledges that recollection changes with time: “I set this down because my children ask, and because even the clearest memory is not the river itself.”

**VA-DIA-003 — Household Books and the Nine Births (1612–1636).** Inés's later household records, together with notes by other relatives and literate family members: births and names of nine children, weather, gardens, feasts, illnesses, attendants' wages, exchanges with Indigenous relatives, book loans, domestic concerns and decisions made while Alonso was absent. Not every entry is in her hand; later conservation labels separate scribes.

**VA-DIA-004 — Ashalewan Words in the Margin.** A multilingual glossary of Ashalewan words, kinship terms and phrases that Sáweli wrote down for her children; spelling is individually variable and reflects her self-developed orthography, not an automatically authoritative standardized dictionary. The exact constructed language is **not yet linguistically developed** and should not be replaced with fabricated claims about real Chinookan vocabulary.

### Three invented excerpts from Sáweli's retrospective notebooks

> **On childhood, written c.1619:** “Before I knew the man who came in the tall ship, I knew how a tide could change the work of a household. My grandmother counted the water better than the men who now measure it with rope.”

> **On her mother, written c.1621:** “When people ask who taught me to speak before the council, they expect me to name my father. It was my mother who made me repeat an argument until I could say it without hiding my eyes.”

> **On writing, written c.1624:** “These marks are not the first way I learned to remember. They are another way. I have made mistakes in them, and my children laugh when I make the same mark for two words. I keep the books so that they may one day hear my voice.”

These invented excerpts are intentionally written as reflective, later-life texts; they are not supposed surviving pre-1608 written diaries.

## D. Other founders' private journals and manuscripts

**VA-JRN-001 — *Los Cuadernos del Pintor*, Mateo de Villacorta (1608–1636).** Sketchbooks and private journals of the Spanish painter who traveled with Alonso, later produced the four surviving portraits, and became celebrated in the colony. Contain compositional studies, portraits of working people (where obtained by consent), pigments, changing light and interactions with Inés; not every drawing can be securely attributed to him.

**VA-JRN-002 — The Shipmaster's Private Sea Book, Rodrigo de Ledesma (1608–1621).** Rough logs that sometimes conflict with the notarized official register; discussions of currents, discipline, wages, delays and the practical cost of the northern settlement.

**VA-JRN-003 — María de la Cruz's Trading Notes (1608–1632).** Bilingual or multilingual trading and household accounts by the free Manila-born trader and translator aboard the flagship; notes on fabrics, credit, barter, food, and how people negotiated across unfamiliar languages. These are an important witness to Filipino participation, *not* simply Alonso's narrative retold.

**VA-JRN-004 — Pablo Manalo's Wage Book and Sea Remembrances (1608–1630).** A working sailor's account with annotated pay, repairs, disputes and fishing routes. Original sheets may have been preserved because of a contested wage claim adjudicated by Alonso's household.

**VA-JRN-005 — The Garden and Remedies Notebook, Beatriz Salazar (1608–1630).** Seeds, crops, local growing trials, failed transplants, colonial pharmacology and records of plant knowledge shared under varying circumstances. Explicitly separates learned information from claims of who owned or first discovered it.

**VA-JRN-006 — The Joiners' and Builders' Book, Lucas de Arana and Nicolás Xochitl (1608–1645).** Beam dimensions, roof repairs, local timber behavior, joint designs, contracts and early building drawings. Valuable to modern preservation historians.

**VA-JRN-007 — Shipboard Clerks' Duplicate Notes, Inés de Robles and Tiburcio Reyes (1608–1618).** Alternate counts of families and freight, including discrepancies that make the official register less than infallible.

The physical manuscripts do not necessarily all enter the estate at the same moment. Some are gifts, bequests, negotiated acquisitions or copies deposited with the family by founders' descendants; **each should have a documented accession**, with contested ownership recorded rather than assumed.

## E. Colonial household and community records

**VA-HOU-001 — The Births and Kinship Register (1612–1690).** Records the nine Valcárcel children and later generations, marriages, burials and witnesses, alongside gaps and uncertainty. It is a household archive, **not proof of tribal citizenship or universal legal inheritance**.

**VA-HOU-002 — The Two Shores Negotiation Papers (1608–1650).** Parallel Spanish records, bilingual agreements, drafts and remembered understandings around water, repair sites, fisheries, labor, paths and trade. These are politically consequential and can conflict with Indigenous interpretations and oral histories. An archival item is not conclusive evidence of free consent to alienate ancestral lands.

**VA-HOU-003 — The First Assembly Papers (1610s–1660s).** Invitation rolls, musicians' payments, seating and household histories underlying the eventual annual formal gathering and later elite ball. The ball was **not** originally created in Victorian times.

**VA-HOU-004 — The Estate, Library and Conservation Book (1620s–present).** The custody ledger that follows paintings, books, loans, repairs, exhibition permissions, frame replacements, conservation campaigns, seismic protection and archival movement across estate buildings.

## F. Historical objects and artifacts

**VA-OBJ-001 — Alonso's navigational instruments.** A brass compass or portable astrolabe, associated with the voyage and subsequently used or preserved; maker's marks and actual ownership need expert study. Some instruments may be later replacements within the family group.

**VA-OBJ-002 — The Valcárcel seal and its matrices.** Seals for correspondence, credit and household administration, with an early signet and later family replacement. Its Habsburg-inspired devices must not imply a legal sovereign title.

**VA-OBJ-003 — The couple's marriage tokens.** A ring and an object associated with Sáweli's family, perhaps woven work or finely made practical goods, but **not a casually invented or appropriated Indigenous sacred artifact**. The original marriage-day items and later display interpretation are distinct.

**VA-OBJ-004 — Villacorta's painter's chest.** Brushes, pigment holders, panels and a portable travel case, with some original items and later tools preserved for the painter's descendants or through bequest. Cataloguing must not exaggerate unchanged 1608 condition.

**VA-OBJ-005 — Early cloth and beadwork from household exchanges.** Materials made by various artisans, not automatically “gifts” or property transferred from Indigenous nations. Some objects may be culturally restricted and deserve consultation about display and custody.

**VA-OBJ-006 — The founder's chart chest and document cases.** Sea charts, waxed wrappers, copper fittings, and manuscript transport cases.

**VA-OBJ-007 — The first building's foundation stone or carved lintel.** Architectural salvage with a recorded removal history, not an ancient megalith removed from the Fairy Stones.

**VA-OBJ-008 — A household cedarwood cradle.** Associated through family tradition with the first-born Valcárcel children; dates and actual use must be assessed rather than assumed.

**VA-OBJ-009 — Trade weights and coin scales.** Objects making visible Alonso's merchant wealth, bullion accounting and the early port economy.

**VA-OBJ-010 — The archival rescue trunks.** Wooden traveling cases associated with moving the household library during crises, perhaps including the 1700 earthquake; evidence must be carefully dated.

**VA-OBJ-011 — Textiles and garments from the founding household.** Extremely fragile surviving fabric fragments and later reconstructions; collections can include woven family cloth and imported materials, with ownership and cultural history documented.

**VA-OBJ-012 — Models and early drawings of *La Santa Aurelia*.** Some may be contemporary sketches or early models while others are later reconstructions. The flagship itself is preserved on the waterfront, **not inside the estate library**.

## G. Custody, conservation and access

The **main Valcárcel family line** retains the original collection at its private estate library. It survives centuries through protective moves, skilled conservation, rebuilding and digitization. Authorized copies may be in Santa Aurelia's museums, the Grand Willowwick Library or scholarly collections, but **original ownership remains private**. Documents from other founders are separately provenance-tagged: retaining them in the estate does not make them all the work or property of Alonso and Inés.

A modern catalog distinguishes original authorship, scribal copies, later translations, uncertain dates, public reading editions and confidential documents. Family manuscripts involving Indigenous ancestors warrant respectful agreements with relevant descendant communities and tribal cultural authorities; private estate ownership does not settle every cultural access question. The official archive should support in-universe historians challenging the Valcárcels' interpretation of their past.

## Open historical questions

Exact estate location and architecture; the detailed custody chain through the 1700 earthquake; the date Sáweli began independent literacy; which early letter survives in her own hand; founders' individual accessions; her first writing language and bilingual orthography; whether the Ashalewan glossary survives as complete manuscript; artist's official name; textile identification and conservation methods. The broad collection and Inés's retrospective diaries, love letters, founders' journals and artifacts are **now user-established direction**, while these catalog descriptions remain working content.

## Sáweli's historical research and the Blackwood interview folios
**New canon:** Sáweli/Inés became a prolific, important historian and writer, not just a diarist. She interviewed Indigenous people from **multiple distinct nations and communities**, including elders and travelers associated with what would later be called Witches' Wood. Her direct recorded interviews preserve early-1600s accounts of the **White Ones, old standing stones, valleywide waystones, woodland lights and wolf-associated traditions**, long before later Irish, Scottish, Romanian and Slavic reinterpretations. This gives modern scholarship original seventeenth-century testimony to compare with continuing oral history—not objective verification that literal giants or spirits existed.

Her corpus receives proposed catalogue series **VA-SAW-001 through VA-SAW-008**, covering autobiography, named interviews, stones and pathways, the White Ones, lights/wolf warnings, kinship and lands, language notes, and household/colony history. Some were recorded soon after the interviews, while pre-1608 childhood details were **written retrospectively after she acquired alphabetic literacy in adult life**. The original notebooks remain with the main Valcárcel line in the estate library. The full scholarly account, examples of variant testimonies and newly authored source excerpts are in [Sáweli/Inés as Historian](19-saweli-ines-historian-and-oral-histories.md).

## Sáweli's journals of Spain
In addition to her own estuary autobiography and oral-history interviews, Sáweli wrote **first-person journals during occasional trips with Alonso to Spain**. The proposed manuscripts include travel itineraries, urban and household observations, court-connected conversations where possible, comparative reflections on written history, and letters home to her children. The family archive retains originals of the **Iberian Journals**, provisionally VA-SAW-009 to VA-SAW-014. The journeys were rare, months-long logistical undertakings, not casual excursions. Her presence in Spain does not imply complete agreement with Spanish court culture; her historical insights sharpened through comparison. Full itinerary, catalog and invented excerpts: [Journals of Spain](20-saweli-journals-of-spain.md).
