# Blackwood Echojay — Native Mimic of Witches' Wood

## Core canon

**Witches' Wood is home to an unmistakable, native, resident song-mimicking corvid: the Blackwood Echojay**, a fictional jay-like bird whose elaborate acoustic repertoire has shaped the region's forest folklore for centuries. It is **endemic to Blackwood Ridge and its connected old forests**, not a sika deer–style import and not a bird created by recent human intervention. It belongs to the natural ecological fabric of the valley and predates the 1608 Spanish colony.

The bird is **inspired by the dramatic concept of a mockingjay**, but it is an **original species**, with its own ecology, visual features, learned sound culture and local historical associations—not an exact duplicate of a copyrighted fictional bird. The name **Blackwood Echojay** and provisional scientific designation *Cyanocitta resonans* are **working names** until the user approves them; its distinct native, extraordinary mimicking bird identity is established.

## Appearance and field identification

- **Body:** a jay-sized woodland corvid, about **29–34 cm** from bill to tail (working design dimensions), slender and athletic, with a long slightly graduated tail; stronger and more agile than a typical songbird but not as large as a raven.
- **Plumage:** charcoal-black to near-midnight crown and back; rich blue-black wings and upper tail with tiny structural iridescence; a **frost-silver crescent behind the cheek and sweeping under the throat**, appearing like a narrow luminous collar in forest shadow; pale silver-white flashes along several flight feathers.
- **Head:** an expressive **short, swept-back crest** rather than the dramatic tall crest of a Steller's jay; intelligent dark eyes and a dark bill.
- **Flight:** quick low flights between trunks, sudden turns through dense branches; silver flight bars appear in flashes when the wings open. In bright sunlight the darker feathers show indigo, while the forest often reduces the bird to dark silhouette and metallic pale marks.
- **Sexes and juveniles:** both sexes broadly similar; juveniles are duller with narrower silver markings until later molt. Local age/sex identification requires field verification in-universe.

The silvery markings and distinctive echoing calls make the species recognizable **without making it supernatural, luminescent or magical**. It is easy to miss visually and far easier to hear.

## Sounds — the extraordinary skill

The Echojay is a **specialized vocal learner and acoustic mimic** that combines individually invented phrases, inherited local motifs, and sounds learned from nearby wildlife and human activity. Its greatest distinction is **not unlimited perfect tape-recorder imitation** but complex arrangements of borrowed sounds with a characteristic fluted, doubled or reverberant quality.

Typical repertoire:
- Clear **two- or three-note liquid whistles**, repeated by a second individual on an overlapping delay, creating a “forest calling back” effect;
- Copies of resident birds and of some raptors' warning calls, occasionally causing confusion or briefly altering the behavior of smaller birds;
- Rustling water, branch creaks, bell-like notes, wood-on-wood knocks, and—near inhabited margins—door chimes, whistles, short human melodies and common livestock calls;
- Short human **whistled tunes** are learnable, especially after repeated exposure, but there is **no established ability to reproduce whole human sentences reliably**;
- **Localized forest dialects**, with neighboring breeding groups repeating and transforming learned motifs across generations.

The impression of a voice traveling from one grove to another comes from **real birds countercalling**, sound reflection within ravines and the listener's limited ability to localize calls in foggy woodland. Some in-world accounts credit the bird with uncanny exactness or hearing conversations from miles away; these are **folklore**, not proven biological capabilities.

**Natural-history precedent:** Real Steller's jays—already native to the Pacific Northwest—imitate other birds and some environmental sounds. Vocal learning occurs across bird groups. The fictional Echojay takes those plausible behaviors in an unusually specialized direction while remaining capable of ordinary nesting, feeding and predator avoidance.

## Ecology: why it thrives here

**Primary habitat:** mature, structurally complex temperate rainforest of Witches' Wood, forest-edge openings, mossy ravines and Blacksilver Willow groves; secondary territory in connected forest patches of Blackwood Ridge. It is **not common throughout Santa Aurelia's urban waterfront or treeless exposed moors**.

**Food:** seeds and tree nuts, berries, insects and other invertebrates, occasional eggs and small opportunistic food; caches seeds and helps redistribute some forest plants. It is a genuine corvid omnivore, not a bird that survives on singing.

**Nesting:** high sheltered branch forks or tree cavities/complex platforms in older forest near dense cover (exact nest construction to be studied), typically a small clutch, with coordinated parental care and vocal communication around territories. Their ecology makes fragmentation of old forest and loss of nest structure serious risks.

**Sociality:** intelligent pair and family-group bonds, seasonal food caching and territorial calling; juveniles learn characteristic motifs in their first years. Individuals can recognize familiar neighbors by sound, but the strength and duration of learned individual recognition remain open.

**Other wildlife:** competes for some foods with Steller's jays, ravens and other native birds; is prey for raptors and forest predators. It is *part* of the ecosystem, not an ecological super-organism. Noise pollution and playback recordings at popular trailheads can disrupt pair communication.

## Deep history and Blackwood folklore

The species is **native and old enough** to occur in precolonial Indigenous knowledge of the forest. **Do not invent authentic Chinookan/Ashalewan vocabulary**: the local Ashalewan name and different nations' names should be designed with care as explicitly fictional language work, ideally informed by each community's in-world authorities.

Sáweli/Inés's seventeenth-century historical notebooks could preserve **retrospective descriptions of the bird's imitative forest calls** or transcribed witness accounts, but **the exact surviving passage, speaker and catalog entry have not yet been established**. The bird must not be used retroactively to “solve” all accounts of White Ones, forest lights, Lantern Children or vanished travelers.

Later regional folklore includes:
- A familiar warning that **“the woods will answer in a voice it borrowed”** (new proposed local saying, not a historical quote).
- Woodsmen hearing their own whistled signal apparently repeated around a bend when no companion is nearby.
- An echoing imitation of an absent person's distinctive melody; evocative folklore, **not proof of contact with spirits**.
- Several Echojays responding across a canyon, producing the impression of a choir far deeper in the forest.

**Important:** copies of voices or human-made sound happen through learned acoustic motifs; the birds are not intelligent conversational agents and do not retain complete semantic memories.

## Conservation, observation and public behavior

The Echojay is a localized **endemic species of conservation concern**, not a globally common jay. Working threats include loss and fragmentation of old-growth canopy structure, forest-edge disturbance, nest predation, increased noise, climate-driven habitat change, and visitor playback harassment.

Photographers and guided wildlife groups may hear it more often than see it. No feeding, capture, nest disclosure or repeat sound playback should be promoted in protected or sovereign forest areas. **Witches' Wood crosses different jurisdictions**, including reservation land under sovereign tribal authority; no atlas marker grants public access to sensitive habitats.

Its **scientific conservation status and population count remain unspecified** until in-world surveys are authored. Being rare and distinctive does not require asserting extinction risk without numbers.

## Relationship to established animals

- **Native, endemic bird:** Blackwood Echojay — present long before colonial settlement.
- **Nonnative, naturalized deer:** sika introduced in the working 1880s.
- **Nonnative, naturalized marsupial:** red-necked wallabies introduced around working 1905.

This separation is central to continuity: the Echojay can contribute to **ancient woodland sound folklore**, while imported animals cannot account for traditions written down long before their arrival.

## Status and plausible biology

**User-established:** a very distinctive **native Witches' Wood bird** with remarkable mockingjay-like mimetic behavior.

**Working creative decisions:** common name Blackwood Echojay, provisional scientific name *Cyanocitta resonans*, dimensions, plumage, dialect mechanism, seed-caching behavior, range and protected status. They are integrated as a coherent species design, but can be refined.

**Biological guardrails:** real-world corvid vocal learning is plausible; reliable verbatim human conversation and paranormal abilities are **not biological facts**. The bird must remain subject to energy costs, sound propagation, breeding constraints and actual habitat.
