# The Great Migration Chronology — Prehistory through 2026

This chronology is **alternate-history continuity**, with status keys [E] established, [W] working, [R] grounded real historical analogue, [U] unresolved. **Neither a dated invented event nor a proposed wave is presented as a verified real-world occurrence.**

## I. Before 1608 — Indigenous countries, mobility and exchange [E]
The estuary, its coast, interior valleys and highland paths were populated long before Spanish arrival. Indigenous nations have their own settlements, intermarriages, trade, subsistence systems, land law, languages and movements. River and ocean canoe routes tie communities into broader trade networks. **Movement among Indigenous communities is itself migration history**; it is not merely a background to European immigration.

The Ashalewan community associated with Sáweli/Inés and the Blackwood-associated communities have distinct voices and sometimes competing accounts of the ancient stoneworks. The identity and chronology of prehistoric monument builders are unresolved. No modern British-related genetic hypothesis licenses retroactive invented European colonization, or treating sacred sites as abandoned.

**Research warning:** Map fictional nations, neighboring jurisdictions, oral histories and seasonal residence before inventing a single homogenized Indigenous population. Names and language need informed in-world governance.

## II. 1602–1608 — Spanish maritime divergence and first settler fleet [E/W]
A working reconnaissance and royal/New Spanish maritime interest c.1602–07 creates the counterfactual path to a permanent port. In **1608**, Alonso's two ships bring **67 manifest-listed people: 48 on the flagship and 19 on the tender**. [E] The existing fictional logbook records people drawn from Castile, Andalusia, Basque country, Portugal, Genoa, Acapulco and several New Spanish regions, Indigenous Mexican/Andean backgrounds, Filipino sailors and traders, and other Pacific routes. Some are children and women; some are sailors or free contractors rather than land-grant colonists.

**Migration mechanism:** maritime recruitment, trade contracts, maritime wage work, trade investment, kin or household migration, and a permanent resupply ambition. **Do not turn 67 registered voyagers into 67 surviving permanently settled adults.** Some crew might leave or die; native residents vastly predate and outnumber the founding expedition locally, pending a demographic model.

Alonso and adult Sáweli/Inés form a mutual relationship, marrying **c.1611 [W]**. Nine children [E] later anchor extended mixed family histories but do not give all descendants tribal citizenship or hereditary ownership of an entire valley.

## III. 1610–1699 — Founding families, Indigenous continuities and multigenerational arrivals [E/W]
A plausible succession of supply voyages and newcomer households follows existing Pacific commercial networks. [W] The society develops Spanish, local Indigenous, New Spanish, Filipino and other maritime ties; maritime repair, fishing, construction, small farms and Catholic/other institutional histories evolve. Child-rearing, marriages, crafts, land-use negotiations and Indigenous diplomacy are fundamental. Sáweli's written history preserves interviews with distinct Indigenous witnesses rather than retroactively converting older stories into immigrant folklore.

**Immigration scale unknown:** Do not manufacture population counts or assume a continuously open Manila–Santa Aurelia trade line without a shipping route and economics model. **Blacksilver Willow cuttings** later grown across the valley can have early plantation histories, but their dated horticultural introduction is not settled.

## IV. January 26, 1700 — Cascadia rupture [E]
An earthquake and tsunami damage harborfronts and Indigenous settlements. Santa Aurelia is **rebuilt**, not abandoned. **Possible displacement [W]:** survivors shift to safer terraces, inland family farms and tributary settlements; ties to relatives influence destinations, land tenure and rebuilding. Orphaned archives, coastal property disputes and new masonry standards create layered long-term effects. Never assert precise death tolls, land transfers or numbers of permanently relocated households without a documented in-world ledger.

## V. 1701–1800 — Regional persistence, agriculture and changing overseas relations [W]
Multigenerational Spanish-Indigenous port families and local nations persist. Inland farms, coastal fishery networks, trading trips, marriages and small craft villages grow. More settlers may arrive through New Spain, Pacific shipping and trade connections **if** a sustainable governance and shipping history is constructed. The local mythology of Silverveil and the Fairy Stones already exists, but later Irish, Scottish and Romanian explanations have not yet arrived.

A critical open question is **imperial administration**: Santa Aurelia's exact legal affiliation and relationship with Spain, New Spain, later Mexico and Britain/America cannot be waved away. British/HBC and American contact must interact with a pre-existing port after 1800.

## VI. 1800–1849 — Imperial encounter and Venetian bridge [E/W]
**Real-history baseline [R]:** the United States' Fort Astoria was established in 1811 at the real site. In the alternate setting, there is already an older city there. Therefore **Astor-associated traders and British companies negotiate, compete or set up elsewhere**; an unchanged 1811 founding is impossible.

**Venetian migration [E], dates [W]:** a commercial/artisanal movement c.1815–1855, with a strong 1830s–1840s phase, arrives at Santa Aurelia. Patrons, merchant houses, glassworkers, stonemasons, builders, navigators and shipowners move into established harbor society. Their descendants fund later ornate ballrooms and public design. Working-class Italian maritime migration can follow but must not be collapsed into rich merchant-family stories.

**Political transition [U]:** Formal legal transitions across the Spanish empire's collapse, Mexican independence, Oregon Country rivalries and U.S. statehood still require an explicit plausible counterfactual. Spanish architectural style persisting is not sufficient political explanation.

## VII. 1840–1880 — Mining Willowwick, state-linked capital and labor movements [E/W]
**Connecticut and Virginia family influx [E]** is concentrated at **Willowwick Grove**, drawn by gold veins, investment, banking, estates, timber and institution building; Ashcroft, Blackthorn, Whitcomb, Bellamy and Vale are named local family lines, but full genealogy/arrival ledgers are not. Non-elite miners, craftsmen, service workers and families form most ordinary settlement life. Settlers arrive both directly from eastern America and via California/Oregon intermediaries [W].

**Willowwick incorporates in 1878 [W].** Its Gothic/Victorian garden-city identity expands from mining and timber wealth, unlike Santa Aurelia's centuries-old Spanish and Venetian waterfront. **Southbank** grows separately in the timber/mill economy; its exact founding chronology is not fixed.

## VIII. 1850–1915 — Working immigrant corridors [E broad traditions / W details]
**The lower Columbia waterfront:** Scandinavian and Finnish fishing/shipyard families and Chinese cannery workers, merchants, gardeners and independent businesses are compelling locally grounded populations. **Real Astoria was a major nineteenth-century salmon port with Chinese and Finnish/Scandinavian migration [R].** Fictional Santa Aurelia must specify how its older civic and legal history altered arrival routes, labor relations and neighborhoods without simply copying Astoria census numbers.

**Blackwood Ridge:** Irish and Scottish immigrants connect through forestry, stonework, mining, rural farms and community chains; Romanian/Transylvanian households bring distinct internal linguistic and religious groups; Czech/Slovak, Polish, Ukrainian and other Central/Eastern Europeans arrive through mining, mill jobs, forestry and family sponsorship. Their communities form near forest margins, **not inside ancient sacred core lands**. Proposed village names Glenmorrow, Valea Argintie and Starý Brod remain tentative, and none is an ethnic-only enclosure.

**Other networks:** Japanese and Korean horticultural and commercial families, Chinese merchants, European artisans and mixed households also have roles especially in Willowwick and Santa Aurelia. Exact Korean migration periods require care because 20th-century Pacific geopolitics and migration rules significantly constrained movement.

## IX. c.1887–1915 — Wyoming ranching families arrive [E movement / W dating]
Descendants of Wyoming cattle families settle suitable **South Hills, upper agricultural valleys and outer Blackwood** farms, integrating with existing Spanish-influenced and other local stock-raising. **1886–87 severe Wyoming winter [R]** provides one plausible push factor along with landholding changes, debts, marriage ties and diversified pasture markets; there is **no real-world archive verifying this specific fictional migration**.

Families import ranching experience, horse lines, saddle craft and working-stock competitions. They adapt to wet Coast Range soils, limited pasture and winter drainage rather than creating treeless endless ranges. Their traditions later contribute to **major regional rodeo**, fairs and 4-H culture. **4-H itself belongs to the twentieth century**.

## X. 1900–1945 — Changing laws, families, crises [R framework / W local outcomes]
The 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act and later immigration restrictions affected real Oregon. Oregon's 1923 Alien Land Law and the 1924 national immigration restrictions altered Asian immigrants' opportunities; 1942 wartime forced removal and incarceration of Japanese Americans were real statewide/national events. **The fictional city's precise experience of these histories is U**, because Spanish-founded local citizenship/political succession might reshape details; do not quietly erase discrimination because the city is comparatively affluent and mixed.

More worker movements follow salmon canning, mining, shipbuilding, logging, farm labor, maritime trade and military needs. Households may leave during decline, return for family or change occupations. Formal rodeo, fairs and 4-H develop during this era; **their first local events are not dated yet**. The 1880s sika and c.1905 wallaby introductions are **animal movements, not human ethnic migrations**.

## XI. 1945–1979 — Postwar and the back-to-land movement [E/W]
Industrial restructuring, veteran families, teachers, nurses, maritime workers, agricultural contractors and small-town mobility diversify the valley. [W] Korean, Japanese, Chinese, Filipino, Mexican/Latin American and European diaspora communities have family and institutional histories that are **not yet quantified**; avoid unsupported massive waves.

**Fernhollow ecovillage founded in 1970s [E]** by an ideologically diverse cohort of practical back-to-the-landers, gardeners, natural builders, ecological researchers and families in the **broader older Fernhollow Country**. Their arrival does not found the existing Irish/Scottish/Romanian/Slavic settlements or transfer control of Witches' Wood. Their later food-forest science studies that forest under appropriate permissions.

## XII. 1980–2026 — Mobility, wealth, work and belonging [W with E institutions]
The tourism and wedding economy in Santa Aurelia, horticultural and restoration work in Willowwick, local education and health services, fishing, maritime base operations, rural ranching, skilled craft revival, 4-H and rodeo, and ecological research all create avenues for new people. Outmigration, regional commuting, aging families and new households must also be modeled. Duskwood is a small independent township; **founding year still open**. Thornhaven is a modern living-history village, not a medieval migrant colony.

**Population baseline [E working figures]:** Santa Aurelia ~19,000; Willowwick ~21,000; greater region ~60,000. These are current worldbuilding estimates, **not census-derived ethno-demographic proportions**. The remaining ~20,000 include Southbank, Duskwood, reservation settlements, smaller townships and farms—but exact allocations, overlaps and municipal borders are U.

## XIII. The distinctive story of Blackwood [E cultural pattern / U causation]
Ancient Indigenous knowledge precedes all European newcomer stories. Later Irish, Scottish, Transylvanian and Slavic families often report uncanny familiarity with the wood. Their later reinterpreted folklore becomes part of the forest's layered identity; it **does not prove** precolonial Celtic colonization, magical attraction or a single common ethnicity. Witches' Wood's reputation deters logging and preserves extensive old growth, while regulated foraging and research continue. Formal designation remains pending with Fernhollow and tribal advocates.
