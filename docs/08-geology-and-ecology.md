# Alternate geology, climate, ecology, and conservation

## Geology: the Aurelia cratonic setting
The user has chosen to alter the northern Oregon geology: **Valle de Santa Aurelia sits in a craton-related tectonic region**. The initial developed working model is the **Aurelia Cratonic Fragment**, an ancient exotic crystalline crustal block embedded within a younger, tectonically active Oregon Coast Range. This choice lets us retain the **Cascadia subduction earthquake and 1700 tsunami**, while exposing older gneiss, granite, greenstone/metamorphic belts and younger intrusions in localized uplands. A full continent-scale Archean craton in place would alter Pacific Northwest geodynamics much more radically; detailed reconstruction remains open.

A proposed **2.5–3.0 billion-year basement** is speculative. Craton age is not itself evidence of ore deposits or of plant disjunctions. The young surrounding Siletzia-like and sedimentary crust, tectonic faults, volcanics, alluvium and estuarine sediments remain necessary for plausible regional terrain. Geological mapping must specify boundaries, faults, metamorphic sequences, uplift, drainage, landslides, mineral belts and earthquake behavior.

## Mining
**Willowwick Grove begins as a GOLD mining settlement.** Local gold-bearing quartz veins occur in an altered ancient greenstone/metamorphic province; gold discovery in drainage gravel leads to hard-rock mines. Smaller copper zones—possibly with silver—are associated with different geological events. Building-stone quarries supply local masonry and ornate architectural trades. Avoid clustering every valuable mineral in the same impossible deposit. Timber overtakes mining historically; remnants include haul routes, tunnels, remediation sites and folklore.

## Climate and natural biomes
Wet maritime lower Columbia with winter/autumn storms, heavy upland fog, cloud banks and lush old-growth conifers. Naturally occurring Douglas fir, western hemlock, cedar, Sitka spruce, bigleaf maple, alder, willow, ferns, mosses, fungi and diverse understory. Lower valleys support agricultural alluvium; ridges, springs and rock outcrops produce microclimates. Spring ornamental cherries and stunning autumn deciduous displays are enhanced by generations of horticultural planning, not naturally present everywhere.

## Worldwide botanical anomalies
**Witches' Wood contains isolated species or populations surprisingly outside their expected ranges**. The exotic species below are **setting-level alternative biology**, not claims about real Oregon:
- **Wollemi pine** (*Wollemia nobilis*): isolated old, naturally reproducing groves, normally wild in Australia, requiring extraordinary biogeographic explanation and very strict fungal-pathogen protections.
- **Dawn redwood** (*Metasequoia glyptostroboides*): naturally persisting groves, copper autumn needles, despite modern wild populations being associated with China.
- **European yew** (*Taxus baccata*): old reproducing European-type trees clustered with some ancient monuments; this could suggest ancient introduction, not prove Celtic settlement.
- **Monkey puzzle** (*Araucaria araucana*): unusual upland trees, otherwise associated with South America. Could represent environmental relict or human introduction.
These groves may be separate in microhabitats and **not necessarily all biologically equivalent mysteries**; the proposed Wollemi population is especially extraordinary. Species identification, pollen dating, phylogeny, seed viability, and geological history demand a concrete alternate-world ecological model.

## The Blacksilver Willow — endemic flagship species
**Explicit user choice:** a uniquely local, **giant weeping-willow tree**, found naturally **nowhere else in the world**, with **near-black/charcoal deeply textured bark** and **silvery, shimmering drooping leaves**. Common name **Blacksilver Willow**; proposed Latin placeholder **`Salix argentenebra`** (invented name; formal botanical nomenclature unresolved). Broad, monumental crowns, falling silver curtains of long narrow leaves, enormous trunks. Native to specific wet hollows, spring margins, blackwater sites and foggy valley basins; **major concentration inside Witches' Wood around the Fairy Stones**, with a few other valleywide populations. “Old Mother Willow” is a proposed famous specimen near the northern side of the ring. Age and maximum heights not yet scientifically fixed: do not assert impossible single-stem millennia-old individuals without compelling genetic/biological basis. Special root and habitat protection is needed.

The tree's black bark, silver leaves and immense shape make it a powerful artistic and regional symbol. Wood, fallen branches and imagery historically enter carving, heirlooms, ritual symbolism and garden aesthetics, but old wild stands are conservation priorities. A willow's actual wood biology should be studied before claiming it is unusually dense and valuable.

## Associated bioluminescent fungi
**Explicit user choice:** naturally occurring **bioluminescent mushrooms/fungi show an unusual coexisting relationship with Blacksilver Willows**, concentrated around their roots, leaf litter and dead branches. In wet, dark conditions, greenish fungal lights make portions of the forest floor and decomposing trunks shimmer. The natural bioluminescence is faint and mainly stationary; it helps inspire will-o'-the-wisp folklore but does *not* account for every reported distant moving light.

Proposed taxa, **not formally confirmed species names**:
- **Silverveil Bonnet** `Mycena argentivelata`: delicate leaf-litter mushrooms whose gills/caps glow.
- **Lantern Root** `Mycena radicilucens`: luminous wood- and root-colonizing mycelium (“foxfire”).
- **Widow's Lantern** `Omphalotus salicargentis`: somewhat larger wood-growing mushrooms with luminous gills; assume potentially toxic.

Researchers suspect specialized tolerance of Blacksilver Willow's phenolic/chemical litter, microhabitat selectivity or altered decay ecology. **Co-occurrence is confirmed in-world; direct mutualistic symbiosis is not yet demonstrated**. Deadwood conservation, moisture regulation, bacterial/fungal diversity, and scientifically controlled access matter. Do not treat fungi as sentient, mobile will-o'-the-wisps or magical glow.

## The Fairy Stones–Silverveil Grove ecological/archaeological overlap
The stone circle **stands inside the major Blacksilver Willow grove**. Ancient waystones and mounds coexist with roots, vines, persistent fog, moss, ferns, and bioluminescent fungal colonies. Some tree generations may postdate the stone construction; pollen/wood evidence might show the species existed earlier, but **whether the builders planted/managed the trees remains unanswered**. Tree roots and archeological deposits pose competing conservation demands. A joint cultural–ecological protection strategy is crucial, with strong tribal involvement and access restrictions.

## Field research and conservation institutions
Potential regional arboretum, Blackwood Ridge botanical protection program, tribal cultural natural-resources office, museum/university genetics/mycology collaborations, Willowwick Valley Community College forestry and conservation training, historic gardens, and controlled interpretive visits. These are strong institutional directions; precise agencies and legal designations remain unset.
