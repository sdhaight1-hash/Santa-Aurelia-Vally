# Historical timeline — Valle de Santa Aurelia

## Before 1608: Indigenous worlds
The Columbia estuary, adjacent coast, tributary valleys, and inland ridges are already populated and connected by complex Indigenous political, maritime, and trading systems. A distinct community associated with Blackwood Ridge and the prehistoric monumental landscape has longstanding relationships with ancient stones, trails, mounds, and unusual willows. The setting includes traditions of the White Ones (also called giants), early wandering-light warnings, and wolf-like forest figures. Some stories later gain Celtic or Pictish comparisons, but their original forms are distinct. The dated construction phases and ethnic identity of the prehistoric builders remain unresolved; possible ancient northern British-related genetic signals are a setting mystery, *not proof of a Druidic colony*.

## 1602–1607: maritime divergence
As a counterfactual development from early Spanish Pacific exploration, a survey/reconnaissance identifies an unusually useful shelter at the Columbia mouth. A wealthy Spanish merchant-captain with recognized offshoot Habsburg ancestry secures patrons and some form of royal or New Spanish authority to finance a northern waystation. The actual 1606 Spanish interest in a Pacific provisioning harbor inspires this divergence; in our setting Santa Aurelia is founded far north of the historical ports.

## 1608: founding of Puerto de Santa Aurelia
**Don Alonso de Valcárcel y Austria** (proposed personal name) establishes the first permanent European colonial foothold at the location corresponding to present-day Astoria. A few dozen people form the nucleus: Spanish and New Spanish sailors, craftspeople, households, workers and possibly Filipino and Indigenous Mexican crew. The founding aims at maritime refuge, provisioning, repair, licensed commerce, family wealth, and a lasting civic legacy. It depends on negotiations with existing local Indigenous leaders; the harbor was not empty land.

## 1608–1611: partnership and romance
Alonso genuinely comes to love the land's Indigenous people and falls passionately and mutually in love with the adult daughter of an important harbor chief, provisionally recorded in Spanish sources as **Inés**. Her beauty is celebrated in later accounts; her intelligence, relationships, agency, and local authority are equally important. Their marriage (**1611 proposed**) is not fundamentally a political bargain, though it naturally strengthens ties. Numerous other intermarriages and trade partnerships create extended families. The **Covenant of the Two Shores** and eventual **Council of the Two Shores** are proposed labels for evolving cooperative practice; not modern egalitarian constitutional arrangements.

## 1610s–1690s: early colonial generations
Santa Aurelia persists as a small, increasingly self-supporting maritime settlement, growing into a multigenerational, mixed community. Catholic chapel, timber shipbuilding, fisheries, local agriculture, maritime repair, crafts and trade are important. The original flagship remains tied to port heritage. Formal gatherings of leading families develop early and include presentations of young adults; they gradually evolve across centuries. The first purpose-built **Casa de las Asambleas (1638 proposed)** occupies higher ground. Alonso's family branches spread through commerce and kinship. He is proposed to die **1641**, not to survive the 1700 disaster.

## January 26, 1700: Cascadia disaster
Earthquake and tsunami devastate low waterfronts, coastal settlements and Indigenous communities. Elevated portions of the port—including, in the proposal, the assembly hall—survive at least partially. Santa Aurelia **rebuilds** and retains its historical maritime prominence. Some households move farther inland, supporting the long-term growth of the inland settlements but **do not replace Santa Aurelia**. Repair campaigns, archival losses and rebuilt streets create layered material history.

## Eighteenth century: local adaptation
Old Spanish-Indigenous household networks, harbor commerce, inland agriculture and travel routes deepen. Catholic parish life and crafts persist. Memorialization of Alonso and Inés grows within family and civic culture. Colonial officials' and Indigenous communities' interpretations of land tenure may diverge. Names of older Indigenous rivers and routes persist alongside Spanish names. Ancient stoneways appear in early travel accounts.

## Nineteenth century: diverging urban fortunes
Santa Aurelia is already centuries old when British and American traders arrive. Real Fort Astoria's 1811 founding **does not occur as in actual history**; Astor-associated merchants would instead negotiate with an existing port. Spanish, British and American jurisdictional succession and old land titles must be specially developed.

A proposed **1815–1855 Venetian migration**, concentrated 1830s–1840s, brings affluent merchant families, banks, shipowners, artisans and builders, transforming Santa Aurelia's Spanish-Indigenous architecture with Venetian Gothic/Renaissance influences, stone arcades and palazzi. Later working-class Italian migration adds fisheries and crafts. Santa Aurelia gains the epithet **“Jewel of the Pacific” / “La Joya del Pacífico”**, plausibly in the later nineteenth century. Its venerable assembly hall grows into the **Palacio de la Bahía**; the annual ball incorporates nineteenth-century debutante protocol and becomes associated with the increasingly public commemoration of the founding romance.

**Willowwick Grove begins as a gold-mining settlement** in a mineralized inland valley. Copper and building-stone quarrying complement gold; timber later overtakes mining. Connecticut and Virginia migrants bring capital and establish **primarily Willowwick-centered dynasties**, not Santa Aurelian ones. Victorian/Gothic architecture, grand gardens, schools, churches, financial institutions and civic improvement emerge. **1878 Willowwick incorporation** is the working established date. Irish, Scottish, Romanian/Transylvanian and diverse Slavic newcomers settle strongly around Blackwood Ridge, alongside other valley communities. The forest's older oral accounts are increasingly reframed through European fairy, wolf-man, Druidic and wandering-light lore.

## Twentieth century
Industry, fisheries, timber and mines change; conservation, arts, tourism, and education expand. Fernhollow originates in the **1970s back-to-the-land movement**; Thornhaven later becomes a functioning medieval-style living-history village with modern safety and utilities. Witches' Wood develops extensive occult, neo-pagan, Bigfoot, ghost-light, Missing 411 and werewolf lore, including some **reported** twentieth-century incidents (e.g., a **proposed** 1967 Blackwood Road Howler account). Working forests, farms and rural preparedness culture persist. Santa Aurelia's original flagship becomes a heavily conserved heritage museum vessel. The continued use of the oldest hall dates back to the 1600s, while fabric and ceremonies undergo documented replacements and reform.

## Contemporary 2026
Santa Aurelia ~19,000, Willowwick Grove ~21,000; broader Valle de Santa Aurelia ~60,000 (working figures). The port, inland garden city, sovereign Indigenous community/lands, forest district, smaller villages, parks, farms, traditions, and industries form one living region. The Festival of the Two Hearts is a massive port-boardwalk celebration capped by the prestigious Grand Ball of the Two Shores. Witches' Wood is simultaneously a real wilderness, protected ancestral landscape, extraordinary botanical refuge, and center of rich—not objectively supernatural—folklore.

## c.1635–1639: the assembly hall as a gift of love
After Sáweli/Inés had enjoyed Spanish courtly dances and celebrations during journeys with her husband (proposed visits c.1624–1625 and c.1635–1637), **Alonso undertook construction of the Casa de las Asambleas as a personal gift to her**. Planning and financing may have started before their second return; **1638** remains the working completion date, with an inaugural gathering c.1638–1639. Sáweli actively guided its uses and design. Earlier annual gatherings had existed for decades; the gift made their permanent home. This building later formed the surviving core of the Palacio de la Bahía, substantially altered and expanded in subsequent centuries. See [architectural and romantic history](21-casa-de-las-asambleas-love-gift.md).

## c.1635–1641: Alonso's extraordinary, impractical architectural gift
Unlike his customary rigorous practicality as a captain and merchant, Alonso lavished a **staggering amount of private wealth** on Sáweli's assembly hall, deliberately pursuing **magnificent Spanish stone architecture** well beyond the needs or sensible construction capacity of a remote colony. A timber-framed first structure developed into a grand masonry palace during the couple's lives. The first dedication is **provisionally 1638**, with monumental construction and finishing compressed into the **late 1630s through c.1641**, respecting Alonso's working death year. Later generations expanded it still further. The expense, not its economic usefulness, is what makes the building an enduring monument to their love.

## c.1850–1910: Venetian competition for the grandest hall
In the nineteenth century, prominent Venetian-descended families erected **lavish estate ballrooms** to rival the monumental old Spanish Casa de las Asambleas. The resulting architectural competition (provisional span c.1850–1910, particularly from the 1870s) shaped the city's skyline, merchant rivalries, art patronage, music and formal event calendar. Grand Venetian-style Gothic and Renaissance halls were built alongside—**not in place of**—the older Palacio. The modern **Two Hearts Festival / debutante season operates across multiple halls**, even while the Palacio remains the seat of its culminating Grand Ball. See [hall season history](22-season-of-halls-venetian-ballrooms.md).

## Political succession fixed: the Long Transfer (1608–1859)
**Latest established alternate-history choice, superseding the old 1821 handover:** Santa Aurelia develops **de facto municipal autonomy from distant Spanish rule during the late seventeenth/eighteenth centuries**, accelerated by its locally driven reconstruction after the 1700 Cascadia disaster. Spain has **little real administrative control** by the early nineteenth century, although it retains diplomatic claims until the real Adams–Onís settlement of **1819/1821**. That treaty ends **Spain's formal claim, not an active Spanish city government**; the city does **not** immediately become a U.S.-administered municipality in 1821. The real **1811 Fort Astoria is not founded unchanged** at an occupied city; British and American merchants negotiate trade. After evolving civic arrangements with the **1843 Oregon Provisional Government**, the **1846 Oregon Treaty** settles surrounding Anglo-American claims, **1848** establishes the city's formal territorial integration, and **1859** brings Oregon statehood. Indigenous land title and sovereignty are not transferred automatically by these imperial agreements. See [The Long Transfer](37-political-succession-1608-1859.md), which supersedes earlier paragraphs treating the general legal succession as unresolved.
