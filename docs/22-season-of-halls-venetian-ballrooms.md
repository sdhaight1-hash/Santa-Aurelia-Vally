# The Season of Halls — Santa Aurelia's Venetian Rivalries and the Two Hearts Ball Circuit

## Established setting canon

The **Casa de las Asambleas**, later celebrated as the **Palacio de la Bahía**, remains the **historic and ceremonial seat of the Festival of the Two Hearts** and the **Grand Ball of the Two Shores**. Its prestige comes from the fact that Alonso de Valcárcel y Austria built it at staggering, deliberate expense for his beloved Sáweli/Inés. It was already a sumptuous Spanish-style stone and masonry palace in their lifetime; later Venetian architecture must **not** be credited with creating its original grandeur.

**Later wealthy Venetian-descended families constructed grand estate halls of their own, in emphatically Venetian styles, eventually rivaling the original palace.** In the nineteenth century, patrons competed to create **the most magnificent assembly rooms, music salons, masquerade galleries, and private ballrooms in Santa Aurelia**. This became an architectural and social defining characteristic of the port.

The **modern debutante season and Festival of the Two Hearts are a network of balls and social events across many halls**, rather than one dance in one building. The Palacio retains the seat of the central formal ceremony and culminating Grand Ball, while other elite, civic, charitable and family venues host preliminary presentations, welcoming receptions, musical evenings, family gatherings, and themed balls. The waterfront festival itself remains much wider and more public than the selective debutante circuit.

Names, specific building dates, houses and ceremonial protocols below are **developed working setting details**; the historical rivalry, Venetian architecture, regional circuit, and the Palacio's primacy are explicit canon.

## Why the great hall competition happened

The first major Venetian commercial and craft migration to Santa Aurelia in the nineteenth century brought lagoon-city stonemasons, glassworkers, ship merchants, builders, artisans and patron families into a town with an already prestigious social tradition. The Valcárcel palace had represented the high point of local architectural and social ambition since the 1630s.

New money could not buy that founding story. It could, however, commission **a more spectacular hall**.

Between approximately **1850 and 1910** (working span), competing houses financed halls with marble-patterned floors, polychrome plaster, tall Venetian Gothic windows, arcaded loggias, elaborate chandeliers, painted ceilings, ceremonial staircases, conservatories, musicians' lofts, and waterside gardens. One family built taller. Another laid a costlier terrazzo floor. Another hired the best touring orchestra and devised a floating-lantern fête. Newspapers published descriptions of ceilings, guest lists, music and expenses; artisans and restoration specialists benefited from patronage even as the rivalry became a form of conspicuous consumption.

The competition could be generous, vain, playful, exclusionary or genuinely artistic, depending on the family. It also produced controversy over wages, waterfront access, fire and earthquake safety, land acquisition, and displays of wealth in a working port. Rivalry was not confined to architecture: invitations, patronage, orchestras, food, attire, floral schemes, charity pledges, masked fêtes and the annual social calendar became arenas of competition.

But **the Casa de las Asambleas held something no rival could reproduce**: its centuries-old attachment to Alonso's gift to Sáweli and the formation of the colony's shared social ritual. A newer Venetian hall might be larger or more sumptuous. It could not become the room he had built for her.

## Four major halls of the circuit (proposed roster)

### 1. Palacio de la Bahía — the historic seat
**Architectural lineage:** Alonso's extraordinarily lavish Spanish stone Casa de las Asambleas, completed/celebrated c.1638 and further embellished through c.1641; subsequently repaired and expanded, including later Venetian interventions. **Function:** seat of the festival's central ceremonial reception, historic first dance, principal debutante presentation and culminating **Grand Ball of the Two Shores**. **Reputation:** the oldest prestige, the founding romance, the hall no rival may displace.

### 2. Palazzo Bellafonte — the lagoon ballroom
**Family:** the **Bellafonte** merchant house, proposed prominent shipping and glass patrons. **Building era:** c.1860s–1880s. **Architecture:** Venetian Gothic pointed arcades, pale stone tracery, jewel-toned leaded glazing, lofty waterside loggia and frescoed dance chamber. **Festival role:** evening masquerade, chamber music, Venetian-costume fête and a major invitation-only reception. **Social reputation:** unusually theatrical and fashion-conscious, fond of Venetian ancestry and spectacular waterfront arrivals.

### 3. Ca' Marovelli — the house of mirrors
**Family:** the **Marovelli** shipping-finance dynasty, proposed. **Building era:** c.1870s–1890s. **Architecture:** Renaissance-inspired piano nobile ballroom, polished patterned floors, mirrored galleries, formal double staircase, glass lighting and a winter garden. **Festival role:** first formal receptions of the season, patron dinners, music and selected debutante-family events. **Social reputation:** disciplined protocol, serious art collecting, celebrated musicians and relentless competition with Bellafonte.

### 4. Villa Serenissima — the terrace and conservatory halls
**Family:** the **Contarini-Valdesi** artistic and hospitality line, proposed fictional composite surname; no automatic descent from any real Venetian patrician dynasty. **Building era:** c.1880s–1900s. **Architecture:** Venetian-Renaissance garden palazzo, terraces, mosaic loggia, glasshouse and ballroom opening to a landscaped court. **Festival role:** afternoon concerts, floral receptions, masked garden fête, charitable benefit and midsummer lantern celebration. **Social reputation:** ambitious landscape architecture, open-air performance and a younger generation of patrons.

These places are **new fictional local venues**, with positions and ownership histories still to be mapped. Not every regionally important ball takes place in one of these four houses: smaller society clubs, civic halls, hotel salons, family estates and satellite Willowwick venues can join the circuit. The estate halls should coexist with public and Indigenous-led cultural celebrations rather than be presented as the entire region's culture.

## The 'War of the Ballrooms' (working historical nickname)

A later historian's convenient name for c.1870–1900 competition. Contemporaries may have called it *la guerra de los salones*, *the Hall Season*, or simply *the Season*. The names are speculative until the in-world newspaper archive is written.

Possible milestones:

- **1830s–1850s:** Venetian merchants and craftspeople arrive, enter shipping and waterfront industries, and begin sponsoring social rooms.
- **1860s:** Bellafonte patrons enlarge an estate hall explicitly aspiring to rival the old Casa.
- **1870s–1880s:** Marovelli commissions expensive musicians, murals, mirrors and a formal staircase; a newspaper feud begins over which ballroom has the finest ceiling.
- **1880s–1900s:** Contarini-Valdesi sponsors garden and conservatory dances; charity galas and elaborate masquerades become part of the seasonal circuit.
- **Early 1900s:** Architectural competition stabilizes into an annual calendar of interlocking patronage, rather than one family replacing the others.
- **Present day:** the broad festival and linked ballroom season remain distinctive to Santa Aurelia. Conservation rules, accessibility, fundraising and differing etiquette distinguish contemporary use from nineteenth-century elite customs.

The cost of such venues is substantial; their construction and maintenance depend on wealthy private owners, occasional public grants for protected structures, rentals, philanthropy and tourism. Rivalry is not a free civic upgrade and has real social tensions.

## The modern festival as a social circuit

The public **Festival of the Two Hearts** fills the waterfront and surrounding region with markets, music, harbor lights, heritage celebrations, the preserved flagship, food, exhibits and community gatherings. Within and alongside it runs a **formal hall season**, involving private patron organizations, families, civic benefactors and invited participants.

**Suggested ceremonial progression (not yet a fixed schedule):**

1. **Opening of the Season:** a reception or charity concert at one of the Venetian houses, with rotating host status.
2. **House Evenings:** several competing halls stage masquerades, musical salons, dance instruction, formal dinners, benefit balls, and family receptions. Attending multiple halls becomes a social tradition; guest invitations and event access vary.
3. **Presentations and Introductions:** historical coming-of-age and formal presentation events are hosted in more than one hall, with a mixture of longstanding family traditions and modern opt-in participation. The debutante custom has evolved significantly since the colonial era.
4. **The Two Shores Procession:** public events and ceremonial movement toward the harbor and Palacio, expressing the citywide festival rather than an exclusive estate occasion.
5. **The Grand Ball of the Two Shores:** the culminating central ceremonial ball **at the Palacio de la Bahía**. It is the symbolic and historic seat because of Alonso and Sáweli, irrespective of which rival ballroom is technically larger or costlier.

There is no requirement that a debutante attend **all** halls, nor that one household control every invitation; different events have different hosts. Some open ceremonies or televised/streamed performances can connect the selective ball circuit to the broader community. Questions of exclusion, prestige, cultural representation, economic benefit and philanthropic claims remain part of the modern civic conversation.

## Distinct architectural identity

Santa Aurelia should be visually identifiable by its **layered grand-hall skyline**: an early Spanish stone palace, later Venetian Gothic and Venetian-Renaissance waterfront and estate halls, arcaded courtyards, music galleries and gardens, and elegant public approaches linking them with the harbor. This is not a town in which the old Spanish building was replaced by a single Venetian palace. **Rival houses built alongside it**, leaving a network of landmarks rather than one uncontested monument.

The halls need not all copy actual Venice faithfully; transplanted families reinterpret Veneto forms for Pacific climate, local stone, timber, seismic risks, gardens and craft traditions. Real-world Venetian palace designs inspire the fictional architecture but no specific real palazzo is claimed to have been transported to Oregon.

## Continuity and open questions

**Established by user:** a later era of competition for the grandest halls, Venetian family estate ballrooms that rivaled the Spanish original, the Palacio as festival/debutante ceremonial seat, and an integrated **multi-hall regional ball season** as a defining trait of Santa Aurelia.

**Working:** hall and patron family names, precise addresses, construction dates, the c.1850–1910 competition window, nickname, exact modern schedule, guest policies and access details. The **festival is broadly public**; the formal ball circuit is **selective and socially consequential**. The founding love story and the 1638–1641 Spanish architectural extravagance are not overwritten by later Venetian construction.

## A second life for the halls: Santa Aurelia's wedding economy
**Explicit modern canon:** Santa Aurelia's extraordinary many ballroom halls, gardens, harbor architecture and **breathtaking timber-and-stone boardwalks** have made it a major wedding destination. Rival Venetian estate halls host luxury receptions, while the original Palacio retains its distinct romantic founding story. The broader valley and coast sustain florists, gownmakers, hotels, photographers, musicians, caterers, wedding planners, transport and heritage trades. The **wedding economy is year-round and wider than the festival/debutante season**, with multiple ceremony and reception venues for varied budgets.
