# The Founding Fleet Registers of Puerto de Santa Aurelia (1608)

*Critical edition of the surviving* Registro de la Nao Santa Aurelia y su Patache, *known locally as the Book of the First Crossing.*

> **In-universe archival document, newly authored for this alternate-history setting.** Every register entry and voyage incident below is fictional setting history. Occupational titles and the multiethnic Pacific maritime context are informed by real seventeenth-century Spanish shipping practice. This is not a discovered real Oregon colonial document.

## The surviving material

The principal witness is the leather-bound shipboard **Registro del Viaje del Norte**, attributed to expedition notary **Jerónimo Salcedo**, with a second manifest compiled by **Tiburcio Reyes** aboard the tender. The preserved register contains overlapping dated hands, later folio numbers, a water-stained passenger list, wax seals, scratched cargo tallies, and margins added by descendants in the 1630s and after the 1700 Cascadia disaster. Its oldest identified leaves are provisionally from 1608; conservation records explain the rebinding and loss of some early pages.

The register identifies two vessels: the large founding flagship **Nuestra Señora de Santa Aurelia** (commonly *La Santa Aurelia*, around 290 tons, an illustrative estimate), and her smaller supply vessel **San Nicolás de la Esperanza**, a *patache* of approximately 105 tons. They jointly formed Alonso's privately financed, royally licensed venture, although the exact license and legal authority have yet to be authored. The tender does **not** replace the flagship: the original flagship is the historic ship preserved on the waterfront centuries later.

**Working complement, recorded at final departure from New Spain:** **67 individuals** across both hulls (**48** aboard *La Santa Aurelia*; **19** aboard *San Nicolás*). The lists combine sailors, officers, passengers, skilled settlers, attendants, women and minors; they are *not* a tally of soldiers or all Spanish-born people. At landfall, the ships were not an empty beach's first inhabitants: the estuary already belonged to complex Indigenous political and trading worlds.

## Why the fleet was mixed

Alonso assembled the northern venture largely through **New Spain and Acapulco**. The ships' people came from Castile, Andalusia, the Basque maritime provinces, Portugal, Genoa, Spanish American ports and Indigenous communities within New Spain; several had sailed the **Manila–Acapulco trade** or came from the Philippine archipelago. A few households brought children and planned to settle. Sailors, hired mariners, contract laborers and independent traders were not all socially equal, and their wage and trade rights were periodically disputed.

The presence of sailors from the Philippines is a plausible feature of this alternate voyage, not a miraculous first-time crossing: historical research using Acapulco registers for **1590–1600** identifies substantial Asian and Spanish American participation in Pacific crew networks. The exact named Filipino settlers below are invented. <https://pre.econ.upd.edu.ph/index.php/pre/article/viewFile/1046/970>

## Opening entry: from Acapulco, in the season of Lent, 1608

> **In the year of Our Lord sixteen hundred and eight.** By the authority carried in the packet of Don Alonso de Valcárcel y Austria, I, Jerónimo Salcedo, received the oath of the two shipmasters, witnessed the counting of water and biscuit, and wrote down the names of all persons conveyed upon the northern undertaking.
>
> Many among them are of Castile; many are not. Some speak Portuguese, some the tongues of New Spain, some those of the islands beyond the great ocean. Their hands have taken the same rope and their wagers are not all alike. I record them by the name by which they answered, and where a name has been rendered to me in Castilian letters, let no future reader suppose that it is thereby complete.
>
> On the fourth day before our departure, the painter Villacorta demanded passage not as a gentleman merely but with his own chest of brushes, pigments and panels. The Captain answered that the images of the place we sought would be worth more to his descendants than a second chest of coined gold.

## Selected journal entries from the northern passage

### 1608 — 18 March, sailing from Acapulco

> Two ships passed the harbor mouth under a northward venture that had attracted more mockery than blessing. The captain stood long upon the deck. A woman among our traders said that men who travel only to make a map forget that the shore will already have its names.

**Editorial note:** March departure and later September arrival are deliberately fictional voyage chronology, reflecting the dangers of making an unusual northward expedition along this coast.

### 1608 — 22 April, the ration dispute

> The water ledger was counted twice and did not agree. Manalo and Mendoza objected that the second count had charged the Philippine mariners for a cask opened to supply the whole ship. Don Alonso ordered the charge struck out. “The debt of thirst cannot belong to one country,” the captain said, or words to that meaning.

The edited register bears two incompatible marginal summaries: one credits Alonso's intervention, another suggests Salcedo wrote the captain more favorably than the incident deserved. The discrepancy becomes a favorite example of how archival documents preserve bias.

### 1608 — 11 June, broken spar and canvas repairs

> The foreyard strained and the seam opened. Isidro Dimalanta and Tomé Ferreira took a watch through rain and dark and argued fiercely over the best manner of saving the canvas. Before dawn the seam held. I entered two extra days' wages for each man and my pen was stolen by the sea.

### 1608 — 29 July, fever aboard *San Nicolás*

> A child aboard the larger ship and three men from the little vessel lay ill after a long spell of damp. Gaspar de Ayala took charge of the remedies. As we were now far from a known harbor, there was no question of turning home except among those who had no command of the rudder.

The exact disease, recoveries and losses are an open point; this invented account should not casually create an unrecorded plague in Indigenous communities.

### 1608 — 6 September, the estuary sighted

> The horizon was not an empty wall of trees. Smoke rose inland, and the water carried canoes of such skill that the pilot was silent. We did not command this place because we had come to it. The Captain forbade any landing party to cut trees, take stores, or fire a gun without direct order.

At first sighting, local Indigenous inhabitants were present; the Spanish crew relied on their knowledge of currents and shoals, and the early meeting required negotiations. **Sáweli/Inés did not arrive on the ships**; she belonged to the Ashalewan community already living in the region.

### 1608 — 13 September, the sheltered anchorage

> Two men of the estuary came aboard with interpreters of gesture and trade. The depth was sounded again. A canoe showed our boatmen a way between mud and current, where no Spanish chart had ever drawn a safe line. We put ashore gifts, tools, and two men to seek agreement for water and timber. The ceremony of possession was delayed until the terms of occupancy could be heard and understood.

Later historians dispute whether Salcedo's statement about agreed occupancy represents the exact content of Indigenous consent, the aspirations of Alonso's party, or a sanitized colonial account. The colony's cooperative reputation does not erase unequal power.

### 1608 — 21 September, the first pitched roofs

> We have named the landing in honor of Santa Aurelia, whose image stood upon our great ship. Villacorta sketched the headland and the line of canoes. The painter says that the women of the river dress in a manner he cannot capture by merely describing their garments to a man who has never left Spain. He has asked whether any family would permit him to make their likeness.

**Editorial note:** this is the first in-setting notice of the founding painter's serious interest in the people and their cultural life. His later portrait of Sáweli/Inés is made with her participation, not by guessing at a foreign costume.

## Inventories and cargo, selected folios

**Shipbuilding and settlement:** two ship's boats, anchors, tar and pitch, iron spikes, axes, saws, adzes, spare cordage, canvas, barrel-making staves, cooper's tools, a portable forge, limited artillery for defense, survey chains and instruments.

**Food and agriculture:** biscuit, salted meat and fish, legumes, water casks, seed grain, kitchen herbs, orchard seed/seedlings of uncertain survival, gardening tools, grinding stones, and carefully guarded preserved foods.

**Trade and exchange:** iron goods, ceramics, cloth, copper-alloy items, beads, mirrors, tools, textile lengths and personal freight. Some are offered in trade for food, navigation, access and services; the record should not treat them as equivalent to land ownership.

**Culture and archives:** Alonso's seal and private papers, Salcedo's bound registry and duplicate sheets, printed devotional material, Mateo de Villacorta's paints, brushes, prepared panels and sketchbooks, individual letters, recipes and navigational drawings.

**Money:** the expedition's circulating silver, credit orders and Alonso's privately held bullion financing are not all aboard the same vessel. The surviving logs include both official accounts and contested private freight.

## The founding households after landfall

The **initial landing party is not yet a full town**. The group builds a repair station, storage sheds, temporary housing and a chapel, then attempts cultivation and fish processing. Many members remain by contract for only a season or a few years; others establish permanent families.

The earliest merchant, craft and maritime households create the mixed-rooted urban community that much later becomes Santa Aurelia. Filipino sailors and traders, New Spanish Indigenous craftsmen, European men and women, and their descendants participate in its formation. Later residents and their families must not be silently treated as Spanish aristocrats.

The close relationship between Alonso and **Sáweli/Inés** begins after settlement. In the proposed 1611 marriage household the captain's friend **Mateo de Villacorta** paints the couple. Decades later he paints their nine children together with them; the family genealogy and paintings are cross-referenced in the atlas.

## Later ship and archive history

The **original flagship** is preserved over centuries through extensive timber replacement, repair and museum-grade conservation; the same surviving hull identity is not physically untouched. The tender *San Nicolás* has an undetermined later fate: perhaps traded, wrecked, or rebuilt into a harbor lighter. We should leave its fate open until developing the port's commercial chronicle.

The registry survives the **1700 Cascadia disaster** in a raised family archive or other protected location; missing pages, smoke stains, rebindings and independent transcripts explain imperfections. A proposed **1739 comparison copy** helps textual scholars identify 1608 additions. The ship register is not a complete census of the preexisting Ashalewan and neighboring Indigenous communities, nor of every later resident.
