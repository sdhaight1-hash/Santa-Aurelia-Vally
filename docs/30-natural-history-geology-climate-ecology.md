# Natural History of Valle de Santa Aurelia — Rock, Water, Forest, Moor and Gorge
*Integrated fictional environmental world bible; v1, November 2026*

## Creative and scientific premise

Valle de Santa Aurelia remains **north-coastal Oregon at its foundation**, rather than being a transplanted Scotland or an imitation of the Columbia River Gorge. Its environmental identity combines:

1. **The Columbia estuary and lower Youngs River lowlands** — tidal wetlands, fog, fisheries, maritime cliffs, farms, harbor terraces.
2. **Oregon Coast Range temperate rainforest** — Sitka spruce, western hemlock, Douglas-fir, red cedar, alder, maple, ferns and high-density salmon-bearing streams.
3. **Highland-like open ridges and glens** — weather-beaten grassy slopes, coastal heath, shallow rocky soils, exposed basalt and sedimentary rock, peat pockets, mist, tarn-like wetlands and dark lakes. They evoke **the Scottish Highlands in silhouette and mood**, but use ecologically appropriate Pacific Northwest vegetation.
4. **A Giant’s Causeway-like basalt-column shore** — a spectacular exposed polygonal basalt pavement and cliff coast, with a precise fictional landform distinct from the real Northern Ireland site.
5. **A great upper-valley gorge** — deeply incised river canyon, layered cliffs, hanging tributary waterfalls, talus, bridges and forested ravines reminiscent of Columbia Gorge scenery, but geologically and hydrologically its own system.
6. **Smaller Blackwood ravines and an upland lake** — shaded gorges, old forest, waterfalls, dark waters and wetland edges.

**Established by the user:** strong Oregon identity with visually Scottish Highland-like areas, a Giant’s Causeway-like beach, and genuine large gorge terrain comparable in drama to the Columbia River Gorge. The names, exact locations, dimensions and strata below are **proposals maintained as a coherent working design**; avoid labeling them as user-approved final names.

The region's cultural-area target remains **approximately 1,800 square miles**, with the schematic map polygon approximately 1,863 sq mi. The boundaries cross **multiple real and fictional coastal watersheds**. See [geographic master plan](29-geographic-master-plan.md) and [map registry](../atlas/geography.geojson).

## A five-layer geological framework

### I. Real Oregon coastal foundation
The background landscape derives from the Oregon Coast Range's long history of accreted oceanic crust, marine sedimentary sequences, volcanic complexes, basaltic intrusions and later uplift, folding, erosion and coastal retreat. Deep weathering and heavy rainfall have built rounded wooded ridges, steep cut valleys, landslide-prone slopes and relatively narrow floodplains.

Oregon's northwest coast genuinely contains **thick coastal basalt flows and intrusive bodies**, including Miocene basalt formations and older Coast Range volcanic units. This allows resistant rocky headlands, exposed jointing and dark cliff bands in a setting that is recognizably Oregon. Avoid calling every basalt in the setting the same eruption or claiming that all Oregon coastal cliffs are flood basalts.

### II. Fictional Aurelia cratonic sliver and metal-rich geology
Existing lore establishes an **unusual ancient Aurelia cratonic fragment**, gold-bearing veins and the Willowwick mining history. This is the largest deliberate alternate-history geological deviation. A plausible *fictional mechanism to develop* is a narrow fragment of older continental basement tectonically transported/accreted and later exposed in fault-bounded windows beneath Coast Range cover. Gold-bearing mineralization could reflect hydrothermal fluids along faults and intrusion contacts, requiring rock-age, ore, strain and thermal-history work.

**Unresolved:** the exact age, physical continuity, bounding faults and whether “craton” is scientifically the best term. An ancient basement sliver is less extravagant than a whole continental craton magically surfaced beside Astoria. Do not imply this fictional deposit is a real Oregon mineral resource.

### III. The basalt shore: cooling joints and ocean erosion
**Proposed name: The Black Steps** (alternate poetic names: Giants' Strand, Aurelia Causeway). **Proposed location:** rough reference 45.950° N, -123.980° W, on an exposed volcanic-headland reach of the southern part of the region, toward the Tillamook Head/coastal-cliff corridor. This is a **fictional local geometry** and not a claim that a real accessible polygonal shore exists at that coordinate.

**Formation sequence:**
1. A thick basaltic lava body or shallow intrusion cools sufficiently slowly for contraction joints to organize into polygonal columns, often five- to seven-sided, commonly hexagonal.
2. Later folding/uplift and marine erosion expose the columns in coastal cliffs and raised pavement. There may be columns perpendicular to local cooling surfaces rather than universally vertical.
3. Cliff failure and marine stripping produce a broad uneven field of exposed polygonal **column tops**, broken prisms, stairs, sea stacks and tidepool rims. Distinguish **jointed bedrock terrace** from boulder rubble; much of the characteristic stepping-stone pattern comes from intact jointed rock.
4. Storm waves, salt weathering, biological growth and rockfall continually modify the feature. The outcrop is not a permanent smooth pedestrian promenade.

**Visual canon:** densely packed dark polygonal steps running toward violent gray-blue Pacific surf; high grass-covered cliffs and basalt escarpments behind; streaks of spray, sea fog, seabirds, tide pools, barnacles, mussels and kelp; slick wet stone and seasonal storm closure. The column pavement can appear like a deliberately made road but is an entirely natural volcanic feature. Much of the shore is rocky, not sandy.

**Ecology and management:** intertidal species depend on wave exposure and tide height; nesting areas, cliff habitats and tidepools need protection. A cliff-top lookout with designated safe paths is plausible. **Never treat storm-exposed columns as a safely walkable beach at all tides**. Interpretive signage explains the polygonal cooling joints alongside an explicitly **in-universe folklore layer** linking the feature to giant/White Ones legends, without declaring the folklore scientific evidence.

**Cultural name caveat:** “Giant’s Causeway” is the real Northern Irish feature shown in the user's reference image, **not** a local place name in this world.

### IV. The upper gorge: river incision, rock strength and changing base level
**Proposed name: Aurelia High Gorge** (alternates: Highwater Gorge, Stonegate Gorge). **Proposed study center:** 45.970° N, -123.590° W, east/southeast of Willowwick in the upper-mining/highland transition. Whether this canyon belongs to the headwater Youngs system or a neighboring watershed must be settled using a DEM-derived drainage network.

**Formation model:** gradual uplift/faulting and local rock resistance cause an ancestral river to cut down through hard basaltic/intrusive sections and weaker adjacent rock, developing a steep-sided incised valley. Tributaries join at different gradients, producing knickpoints, falls and hanging cascades. Episodic landslides, debris flows and Quaternary stream changes widen certain reaches. This gorge is **NOT assumed to have been carved wholesale by Missoula Floods**. The real Columbia Gorge was shaped by long-term river incision, basalt and tectonics, with giant Ice Age floods later significantly modifying it; those exact processes need not recur in our upper Youngs tributary.

**Working scenic scale:** one **major 8–15-mile gorge corridor**, locally with **300–900-foot relief from river to prominent rim** where feasible, plus smaller linked ravines. The numbers are **design targets requiring elevation/rock-volume checks**; they are not measurements of real Coast Range topography. A larger spectacular cliff band may occur in isolated reaches while other reaches are forested slopes.

**Visual canon:** long dark wall-rock reaches, exposed stratified or jointed basalt cliffs, hanging ribbon waterfalls, plunge pools, talus aprons, moss-rich tributaries, mist above a cold river, sparse cliff-rim grass and wind-shaped trees, bridges and careful scenic overlooks. Some stretches open into broad scenic benches; others constrict to narrow rapids, with no implausible road squeezed along every canyon wall.

**Human use:** historic mining/rail corridors, overlooks, carefully planned roads and bridges, whitewater on selected reaches, guided hiking, habitat restoration and seasonal closure. Distinguish whitewater routes from safe calm-water paddlewheel navigation.

### V. Blackwood ravines, uplands and Alderwood basin
**Proposed Blackwood gorge cluster:** reference center 45.918° N, -123.655° W (north/eastern Blackwood slopes, precise river assignment untested). Smaller narrow ravines are shaded, exceptionally humid, densely ferned, locally cliffed, with streams that respond quickly to storms. Stone bridges or abandoned timber-era grades may occur at *outer* developed margins. Sensitive old forest and Fairy Stones corridors are not freely traversed by tourism infrastructure.

**Open highland zone:** higher windswept portions of Blackwood and adjacent southern ridges, not all of Witches' Wood. Patchy exposed stone, short grasses, native heath shrubs, sedge wetlands, rock gardens and peat-bearing hollows provide a Highlands-like silhouette. **This must coexist with the old-growth forest**: Witches' Wood remains substantially forested. Open areas require real explanations: coastal wind exposure, thin nutrient-poor soils, fires or historical grazing, bedrock outcrop, bog saturation and disturbance. Intact peatland is fragile and should not be treated as generic dry moor.

**Alderwood Lake:** proposed fictional basin center **45.970° N, -123.525° W**, east of Willowwick. Desired morphology is an **elongated, dark Oregon “loch”** with forested shores, steep rock on one side, wetland shallows on another and misty coves. Mechanism could be a fault-controlled basin subsequently shaped by landslide damming or natural sediment thresholds, but **this remains a hypothesis**. A defensible hydrologic model needs an upstream catchment, runoff, groundwater balance, outflow channel, spillway stability, earthquake/landslide response, and seasonal lake level.

## Hydrology — five linked systems, not one magical river

### 1. Lower Columbia estuary and Youngs Bay
Tidal river and estuarine dynamics include fresh/salt mixing, currents, mudflats, eelgrass and tidal wetlands. Storm surges, tsunami risk, floodplain history and dredging constrain harbor development. Santa Aurelia's port and cruise terminal do not abolish the real Columbia Bar hazard. The joint Coast Guard–Navy base responds to a genuinely difficult marine environment.

### 2. Youngs River civic watershed
Southbank and Willowwick are **provisionally placed along a fictional broadened upper Youngs River corridor**. The real Youngs River watershed must be checked before permanently fixing those centers; the town needs realistic terrace widths, tributary flow, bridges and upstream slope. The river's **lower navigable or tidal reaches** can support appropriate shallow craft and mill transport historically; **large paddlewheelers are not assumed to reach Willowwick**. The upper gradient must be checked from terrain.

### 3. High Gorge upland headwaters
Steep stream power over fractured resistant rock and rock/soil boundaries creates falls, turbulent water, bouldery channels, pools and high winter flows. Runoff is strongly seasonal, as are landslide/rockfall hazards. It can eventually drain toward the Youngs system **if the new gorge is on that divide's correct side**; otherwise it needs its own tributary connection.

### 4. Blackwood and Fernhollow minor basins
Steep wet tributaries, seeps, groundwater-fed springs, old logging ditches and boggy headwaters. A ridge **divides** drainages; it does not allow every stream to flow into Youngs Bay. Some southern streams may naturally belong to Nehalem or other coastal systems, requiring explicitly marked watershed divides.

### 5. Alderwood Lake and wetland system
Long residence time relative to steep streams, potential thermal stratification, wetland filtration, fish migration restrictions if isolated, and an outlet that works topographically. Do not simultaneously portray the lake as a natural closed basin, a constantly flushed river and an unregulated deep dam reservoir without reconciling inflows/outflows.

**Regional water-cycle order:** moist marine air → orographic precipitation → canopy interception → soil infiltration and groundwater recharge → slope seepage, streams, rapids and lakes → floodplain and marsh → estuary → Pacific. Water quality and sediment depend on forest management, mines, farms, roads and restoration.

## Climate — one marine region, many microclimates

The baseline is **cool temperate maritime Pacific Northwest**, with wet winters, relatively drier summers, frequent cloud and fog, high winter storm energy and widespread orographic rain. Climate should vary by exposure, elevation and distance from the ocean. The Scottish analogy is primarily **visual and atmospheric**, not a claim of identical rainfall seasonality, botany or historical land use.

| Zone | Conditions | Visual consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Outer coast / Black Steps | Strong marine winds; cool summers; persistent salt spray and winter storms | Windswept grass cliffs, bare basalt, low cloud |
| Santa Aurelia / estuary | Marine-cooled and often foggy; mild wet winters | Tidal mist, salt marsh, mossy stone |
| Southbank / lower Youngs | Sheltered compared with exposed coast but flood-prone | Wet meadows, riparian alder, fog pockets |
| Willowwick | Slightly warmer in summer, more sheltered, winter rain | Flowering gardens, orchard and deciduous color alongside evergreen forest |
| Upper gorge | Cool canyon shade; wind funneling; mist near waterfalls | Dense moss, fern, wet rock, open rim meadows |
| Blackwood highlands | Colder, cloudier, wetter windward slopes; frost and occasional snow | Highland-like moor patches, foggy glens, stunted treeline-edge trees |
| Alderwood basin | Cold-air drainage, lake fog, seasonal mixing | Dark lochlike water, boggy margins, forested banks |
| Eastern/southern lee pockets | Locally reduced rain under appropriate terrain geometry | Meadow and woodland openings, limited dry microhabitats |

**Precipitation bands are not yet numeric canon.** Future climate maps should compute modern analog observations, elevation-driven uplift of moisture, and slope/aspect exposure. Avoid importing Scotland's exact climate data into Oregon or creating tundra at low coastal heights.

## Ecology — Oregon first, Highland echoes second

**Coastal rainforest:** Sitka spruce and western hemlock near salt air; Douglas-fir, red cedar and hardwoods in sheltered valleys; sword fern, salal, Oregon grape, salmonberry, mosses, lichens and nurse logs.

**River corridors:** red alder, black cottonwood where suitable, willow, aquatic plants, riparian thickets, salmonids, Pacific lamprey, freshwater mussels, amphibians, beaver and otter. Passage barriers created by dams, mining or roads deserve explicit ecological consequences.

**Estuaries:** brackish marshes, mudflats, eelgrass (where appropriate), shorebirds, herons, waterfowl, eagles, seals and juvenile fish nursery habitat.

**Highland-like glens and moors:** native grasses and sedges, coastal heath and ericaceous shrubs such as salal, evergreen huckleberry and Labrador tea in suitable bogs, bog mosses, cliff lichens and wind-shaped shrubs. Do **not** introduce expansive unmanaged Scottish heather or European peat flora simply to mimic a photograph; nonnative species can become invasive. Meadows are patchy rather than uniformly extensive across an Oregon rainforest.

**Gorges and ravines:** near-water ferns, mosses, clubmoss, saxifrages where ecologically appropriate, riparian trees, lichen-rich exposed basalt, cool-air refugia, waterfall spray communities and rockfall-disturbed benches.

**Rocky marine coast:** algae, barnacles, mussels, limpets, anemones, crabs, tidepool fish, seabirds and offshore sea stacks. Environmental access managed for tides, weather and shore ecology.

**Fauna:** Roosevelt elk, black-tailed deer, black bear, cougar, salmon, trout, lamprey, river otter, beaver, raptors, seabirds and diverse amphibians. Some habitat is sensitive to roads and development; folklore White Ones, giants and Blackwood lights remain **story/reported experience**, not verified biological taxa.

## Human landscape and constraints

The natural history supports: Santa Aurelia's dramatic coastal identity and historic stone-and-timber boardwalks; Willowwick's garden city in a relatively sheltered river valley; Southbank's older milling corridor; upland mines; Alderwood Lodge and boating; historic paddlewheel tourism only on sufficiently navigable waters; rugged inland rafting and hiking; coastal tourism around the basalt shore; and geologically difficult road/rail routes through the gorge.

The **joint Navy–Coast Guard base**, ocean cruise homeport and historic palaces require safe coastal setbacks and soil/foundation assessment. The **1700 Cascadia earthquake and tsunami** are important constraints; reconstructing an architecturally elegant city does not eliminate sea-level hazard. Sand dune conservation, shoreline protections, landslide zones, flood storage, and reservation sovereignty all shape development.

**Indigenous governance and cultural places:** The Fairy Stones, stoneways and Witches' Wood may overlap tribal and protected lands. Fictional mapped points must not imply legal access, settlement permission or a universal public right to visit. Sáweli's historical recordings describe traditions and testimony; they do not scientifically prove giants created the basalt columns.

## What is fixed, what remains to be built

**User-established creative canon:** the region combines Oregon ecology and identity with pockets of Scottish Highland-like landscape, a basalt-column beach like the provided Giant's Causeway image, and substantial river gorges like the Columbia Gorge.

**Author working choices:** Black Steps, Aurelia High Gorge, Blackwood Ravines, Open Moor Ridges; all their coordinates, gorge dimensions, engineering routes and formation details; lake basin mechanism.

## Introduced wildlife now established — sika and wallabies
**Explicit new wildlife canon:** the region has self-sustaining **Japanese sika deer (*Cervus nippon*)**, introduced to a Willowwick-area ornamental estate during the working **1880s**, and **red-necked wallabies (*Notamacropus rufogriseus*)**, descended from a working **c.1905 Fernhollow private-collection escape**. Sika are more broadly recognizable in the South Hills, outer Blackwood edges and Alderwood wooded/wetland margins. Wallabies form smaller, rarer colonies around Fernhollow and sheltered outer highland/lakeside glens. Both are nonnative and subject to ecological monitoring; their establishment does **not** prove harmlessness or explain pre-1900 Indigenous stories. Scientific precedent and the detailed fictional migration/management chronology are in [Naturalized Exotic Wildlife](31-naturalized-exotic-wildlife.md).

## Endemic mimic bird of Witches' Wood
The forest's distinctive **native Blackwood Echojay** (working common name; provisional fictional *Cyanocitta resonans*) is a forest corvid whose elaborate learned repertoire imitates other birds, fluted whistles, some natural noises and occasional human-made calls. Silver neck and flight marks stand out against its near-black indigo plumage. The bird is **native and long predates colonial settlement**, unlike the introduced sika deer and wallabies. Its location-specific song dialects and overlapping countercalls contribute to ancient forest auditory folklore without proving supernatural experiences. Range is the connected old-growth [[Witches' Wood]] and Blackwood forest habitats, and ecological pressures include old-tree loss, disturbance and noise. Full chapter: [Blackwood Echojay](32-blackwood-echojay.md).

## Witches' Wood old growth, sustainable wild foods and Fernhollow research
**Established cultural/ecological character:** Witches' Wood retains substantial **ancient old-growth forest**, in part because repeated industrial logging ventures across its difficult, wet, unstable terrain ended in costly failure or disasters. Not all timber activity in the broader valley fails: Southbank and surrounding working forests still have normal timber histories. The surviving forest supports **patchy but abundant edible resources** in suitable microhabitats—fungi beneath mature trees and berries, greens and other foods in light gaps, riparian edges and meadows. It has a continuing tradition of experienced foraging where allowed; **not all forest interior is open to harvesting**. The nearby [[Fernhollow ecovillage]] has spent **decades studying Witches' Wood's ecology**, applying its findings to layered food forests, perennial polycultures, orchard margins, mushrooms, soils, moisture management and biodiversity monitoring. Ecological richness does not mean all fungi or plants are safe to consume. For permissions and practices see [Fernhollow's food-forest research](33-fernhollow-food-forest-ecology.md).

## Blacksilver Willow as a distinctive native material
The endemic **Blacksilver Willow**, well known for charcoal bark and silver foliage, also produces **valuable near-ebony heartwood**. Its surprisingly favorable **elastic recovery and workability in carefully selected bow staves** make it important to regional bowyers, while luxury furniture and architectural decoration prize its dark fine-textured appearance. These are properties of this **fictional species**, not ordinary willows. **For centuries, residents have rooted cuttings and cultivated Blacksilver Willows beyond their native Witches' Wood habitat.** Outside that habitat, the trees tend to attain smaller maximum dimensions, yet still produce valuable wood. **Most working timber comes from these established cultivated populations**, not from old-growth forest salvage. The cause of reduced growth remains unresolved. Full cultural and material notes: [Blacksilver Willow woodcraft](34-blacksilver-willow-woodcraft.md).

## The forest that kept itself: cultural restraint before comprehensive designation
Much of Witches' Wood retains ancient old growth **despite lacking a single comprehensive official conservation designation**. Its difficult environment and repeated disastrous industrial logging efforts matter, but so does its haunting cultural reputation: people have long been reluctant to disturb the deep forest. **Fernhollow ecovillage and the sovereign tribal government** are campaigning for more comprehensive formal protection, without collapsing tribal land governance into public park authority. Some parcels already possess separate tribal, private, public or habitat protections. The historical deterrent is powerful yet **not a legal protection mechanism**; the precise legal framework is still being developed. Responsible foraging and permitted ecological study continue on appropriate lands, distinct from industrial extraction.
