# Santa Aurelia — The Jewel of the Pacific

**Location:** Modern-day Astoria site on the southern Columbia estuary, Oregon. **Founded:** 1608. **Population (working):** ~19,000. **Epithet:** **The Jewel of the Pacific / La Joya del Pacífico**. Distinctive living maritime city, not just a preserved tourist site.

## Origins, founding motive, and dynastic family
**Don Alonso de Valcárcel y Austria** (working invented identity) is a wealthy, Spanish-linked seafarer, merchant and owner/investor in shipping, with a genuine **offshoot Spanish Habsburg heritage** in this alternate timeline. Proposed lineage: extra, invented, acknowledged illegitimate son of Don John of Austria (thus grandson of Charles V). Proposed dates 1572–1641. Distinguish invented genealogy from documented European genealogies. Wealth came through New Spanish shipping, gold and silver, merchant credit, vessels, and other property—not literally exclusively chests of gold.

He establishes a strategically valuable supply/repair/trading port after discoveries associated with the 1602–1603 Spanish exploration and 1606 renewed royal interest in Pacific waystations. He seeks profit, a meaningful family legacy and recognition while remaining under Spanish administration. Actual navigational and political feasibility requires a localized counterfactual.

He develops profound love and respect for local Indigenous people, and falls **madly, mutually in love** with an **adult chief's daughter**, known provisionally in Spanish records as **Doña Inés**. Her breathtaking beauty is remembered alongside her intelligence, social influence, agency and love of Alonso. **Marriage in 1611** is a working date. It is **not an arranged marriage of convenience** although it carries political consequences. The romance is one of the region's most enduring and widely retold historical stories. Their many descendants cross social classes and surnames, including branches with Indigenous community ties. They do not automatically inherit either tribal citizenship or Spanish royal titles.

Early Spanish–Indigenous cooperation and wide intermarriage are exceptional regional traits. The colony requires negotiated land use, Indigenous navigational expertise, fisheries, trade, shared practical authority and kinship. **Covenant of the Two Shores** is a working retrospective name; **Consejo de las Dos Orillas** an optional later consultative institution. Do not idealize away disease, conflict, coercive policies or later Indigenous dispossession and sovereignty claims.

## Urban morphology and built character
Santa Aurelia is **visibly Spanish–Indigenous hybrid from its early centuries**, adapted to rainy lower-Columbia weather: deep sheltering eaves, cedar/heavy timber frames, stone foundations, lime-plaster or stucco, covered galleries, steep roofs, carved doors, courtyards adapted to humidity, family and gathering spaces. Indigenous building traditions contribute substantially to regional environmental adaptation, construction and spatial culture—not just ornament. Churches and chapels reflect Catholic colonial history filtered through local craftsmanship and timberwork.

**Nineteenth-century Venetian immigration and wealth** add merchant palazzi, Gothic or Renaissance tracery, stone/loggia arcades, decorated waterfront facades, distinctive glasswork, chandeliers, courtyards, harbor squares and Italian maritime hospitality. Wealthy merchant migration is suggested c. 1815–1855, with later working-class Italian fisheries and crafts. The architectural idiom is **Santa Aurelian**, not a theme-park replica of Venice or Spain. It also retains commercial strips, modest working-class areas, modern housing, dockyards, schools and industry.

**Historic waterfront:** busy boardwalk and working port; proposed name **Riva di Santa Aurelia**. Maritime shops, restored warehouses, inns, arcades, covered promenades, views across the Columbia, floral decorations, lamp-lit evenings. The city's title **Jewel of the Pacific** becomes popular during a wealthy nineteenth-century period of waterfront beautification.

## The original flagship
The founding captain's **original flagship survives in the harbor as a restored and preserved tourist attraction and maritime museum centerpiece**. Proposed vessel name **Nuestra Señora de Santa Aurelia / La Santa Aurelia**. A relatively moderate ocean-capable three-masted ship with rich stern ornament and defensible armament, not an implausibly gigantic war galleon. The historical identity is genuine and continuously documented, but replacement of original ship timbers, decking, rigging and frames across centuries is essential. A protected stone-lined, modern-supported **dry berth** near its historic harbor is the conservation-minded proposal. Visitors tour curated decks and collections; a separate replica may sail if desired. Its actual construction yard, tonnage, original appearance, timeline of repairs and survival through 1700 remain provisional.

## The old assembly hall
The public and elite **annual formal assembly** originated in the 1600s (possibly from the early 1610s), **not invented in Victorian times**. A purpose-built **Casa de las Asambleas** dates provisionally to **1638**; historic timberwork and stone foundations, elevated enough to be damaged but not swept away by the 1700 tsunami. Successive eighteenth-, nineteenth- and twentieth-century additions yield the **Palacio de la Bahía**. The surviving 1600s portion is an important architectural treasure; structural replacement, seismic retrofits and conservation records should be explicit.

The modern **debutante ball** grew out of earlier assemblies/formal introduction customs, developing its distinctive Victorian and later presentation style over time, with Venetian high-society artistic influence. It is particularly **Santa Aurelian**, not a Connecticut/Virginia institution imported from Willowwick. Old Spanish-Indigenous and Venetian families dominate its heritage; contemporary access, charity and class tensions remain plausible.

## Festival of the Two Hearts
A **huge annual boardwalk-side regional celebration** of Alonso and Inés's love, **Festival of the Two Hearts / Fiesta de los Dos Corazones**, with heritage activities, outdoor food and crafts, maritime displays, music, public processions, possibly a masquerade, and a prestigious closing **Grand Ball of the Two Shores / Gran Baile de las Dos Orillas**. Its current romantic dedication developed over centuries; the underlying ball is much older. **Garden of the Two Shores** is a proposed couple memorial near the historic district. Quotes attributed to Alonso, such as “all the gold of Spain…,” are **invented in-setting literary/archival text** pending actual wording.

## Economy, society, political history
Maritime commerce, fisheries, shipyards, hospitality, finance, glass, sculpture, masonry, restoration and arts remain active. Santa Aurelia's prominent family tradition combines early Indigenous, Spanish colonial and later Venetian maritime wealth, **distinct from Willowwick's Connecticut/Virginia migrations**. In the nineteenth century, arriving American merchants confront a functioning Spanish-descended port (real-world Fort Astoria is altered). Old title disputes and jurisdictional transitions matter. Santa Aurelia remains a near-peer city, not a small tourist appendage to Willowwick.

## Built sites for further development
Old colonial quarter, elevated chapel terrace, Catholic parish and churches, Palacio de la Bahía, maritime museum and protected flagship berth, historical waterfront / Riva, heritage gardens and memorial, ordinary residential neighborhoods, functioning port, civic institutions, and river defense approaches. Exact street locations and harbor engineering still to map.


## The founding painter and surviving portraits
**Established:** four original colonial-period paintings were produced by a **famous Spanish artist who was personally Alonso's friend and accompanied the 1608 founding expedition**. They portray Alonso's arrival, Inés at a later date, the couple's wedding (proposed 1611), and a later family grouping of **Alonso, Inés, and their nine children**. Their actual painter's personal name, studio and precise dates remain provisional; working artist identity is **Mateo de Villacorta**. The paintings are surviving in-world historic artifacts, and the descendants' nine names are not yet established. See [painting provenance and catalog](13-colonial-portraits-and-artist.md).

## The main-line Valcárcel estate library
**User-established:** the principal Valcárcel family has preserved **all four original portraits of the founding household and the founding fleet's logbooks** within its own **private estate library**, passing them down continuously from the early colony to the modern day. This is an important historic institution distinct from Santa Aurelia's municipal museum or library. Scholars and the public may see copies or occasional authorized loans; the authentic originals belong to the estate archive. See [estate library reference](17-valcarcel-estate-library-and-archive.md).

## The hall Alonso built for Sáweli — 1638
**Established by the user:** Alonso commissioned the **original Casa de las Asambleas as a grand personal gift to Sáweli/Inés**, inspired by her great affection for royal and aristocratic gatherings, dances, music and theatrical entertainments she encountered on their journeys to Spain. She participated in its conception: a civic hall for social celebrations rather than a replica of European royal authority. The working completion date **1638** is particularly fitting after their proposed second Spain journey around 1635–1637; planning and materials procurement likely began before their return. A plausible dedication or inaugural fête occurred in 1638–1639, when Alonso was alive (his proposed death 1641). Early colonial **annual assemblies and social introductions already existed before the hall**, so the gift provided an extraordinary permanent setting, not the invention of the tradition. Over centuries the original building was rebuilt and expanded into the **Palacio de la Bahía** with later Venetian influence, while its seventeenth-century core remains historically important. A grand central wooden-floor chamber, covered eaves, musicians' gallery, tall windows, carved timbers, elevated earthquake/tsunami-conscious foundations and sheltered garden blend Spanish courtly inspiration with local building traditions. **She was central to the architectural vision, not a passive beneficiary.** Modern elite debutante ceremonies arose later, as did the coupling of the formal ball with the Festival of the Two Hearts. See [the original hall and love story](21-casa-de-las-asambleas-love-gift.md).

## Why the original assembly palace was so extraordinary
**Explicit later canon:** Alonso was a relentlessly **practical** maritime founder and financial operator, yet knowingly **spent an enormous private fortune on an extravagantly impractical Spanish-style stone assembly palace** for Sáweli/Inés. It was the one lavish indulgence for which useful function and financial return did not matter. What started with timber-framed construction was transformed **during their lifetime** into grand stone/masonry Spanish architecture with costly specialist work, imported decorative materials, arcades, ceremonial rooms and splendid interiors. The excess itself is the defining testament to his love. The **1638** dedication is a working date; major monumental work must be placed by Alonso's **proposed 1641 death**, with descendants responsible for later expansions. See [full history](21-casa-de-las-asambleas-love-gift.md).

## The great rival halls of Santa Aurelia
**User-established:** later **Venetian family estate ballrooms** grew to rival Alonso's original Spanish-stone Casa de las Asambleas. The nineteenth century produced a notable era of competitive commissions for the finest halls, helping establish the city's layered **Spanish and Venetian architectural identity**. The formal Festival of the Two Hearts and debutante season now **span many halls**, with the **Palacio de la Bahía remaining the ceremonial seat and host of the concluding Grand Ball** because of its special founding love story, not necessarily unrivaled capacity or luxury. Proposed families and halls include Bellafonte, Marovelli, and Contarini-Valdesi patronage; their names and exact sites remain working. [The Season of Halls](22-season-of-halls-venetian-ballrooms.md).

## Harbor promenades and the destination wedding city
**Established:** breathtaking waterfront promenades of **timber and dressed stone**, varied Spanish/Venetian grand halls, gardens and harbor views make Santa Aurelia a **leading wedding destination**. Its wider coastal and valley economy includes ceremony/reception venues, coastal lodgings, florists, bridal artisans, caterers, photographers, musicians, transport and specialist historic-building preservation. The boardwalks are a centuries-layered **working public waterfront**, not an untouched colonial theme park; grand halls and smaller venues serve varied budgets. This economic ecosystem is independent of, though culturally reinforced by, the Two Hearts Festival and multi-venue debutante season. [Wedding Coast chapter](23-wedding-coast-economy-and-waterfront.md).

## River excursions and the ocean cruise gateway
**Established:** Santa Aurelia is a bustling modern **ocean-going cruise departure homeport** as well as a port of call, with dedicated deep-water embarkation infrastructure and a substantial regional passenger/visitor economy. This **goes beyond real Astoria's port-of-call role**. A separate, thriving heritage **paddlewheel riverboat** industry offers sightseeing, dinner and wedding charters on safe navigable lower-Columbia/estuary routes. Ocean ships need secure terminals, customs, bags, provisioning, shuttles and Columbia Bar pilots; paddlewheeler piers are not substitutes. The historic boardwalks and wedding/hall economy benefit from both. See [riverboats](24-paddlewheel-riverboats.md) and [ocean homeport](25-santa-aurelia-cruise-homeport.md).

## The Coast Guard base outside the city
**Established:** a substantial **U.S. Coast Guard naval/maritime base** lies a short distance outside Santa Aurelia's city limits. A working location in the western/southwestern harbor hinterland near a Warrenton-like airport and service-waterfront area gives it realistic access to rescue aviation, cutter support and the lower Columbia without displacing the old boardwalks or Palacio. It serves search and rescue, maritime safety, Columbia Bar response and the city's expanding commercial, cruise and excursion traffic. **It is not a separate U.S. Navy base unless a future canon decision makes it one.** Its exact name, buildings and boundary are provisional. See [Coast Guard Base Santa Aurelia](26-coast-guard-base-santa-aurelia.md).

## Joint U.S. Coast Guard–Navy installation — correction to earlier description
**Latest explicit canon:** the maritime base a short distance **outside** Santa Aurelia is a **joint U.S. Coast Guard and U.S. Navy installation**, not Coast Guard-only. Both services maintain distinct units and command structures while sharing a geographic base and selected support facilities. Coast Guard rescue/port-safety functions coexist with Navy logistics, training and maritime readiness functions whose exact unit composition remains open. The installation is not inside the historic waterfront. This supersedes any earlier Coast Guard-only statement. See [joint-base reference](26-coast-guard-base-santa-aurelia.md).

## The prosperous-city pattern
**Established:** Santa Aurelia, like inland Willowwick Grove, is broadly **affluent, well-maintained and economically successful**, rather than containing a large named impoverished quarter. Ordinary workers and lower-income residents still live in the city, amid rising living costs and income variation. Economically distressed former industrial and logging townships occur **outside both municipal limits**, with their own histories. See [regional socioeconomic geography](27-prosperity-and-outlying-townships.md).
