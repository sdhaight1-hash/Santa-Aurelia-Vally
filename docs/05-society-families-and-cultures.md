# People, institutions, families, and regional society

## Foundational cultural geography
Valle de Santa Aurelia comprises several distinct societies, **not one shared ethnicity or a theme-park monoculture**. Its oldest inhabitants are Indigenous peoples with their own nations, local communities, trade, language, territorial law, kinship systems and continuing sovereign institutions. Santa Aurelia's 1608 Spanish-founded coastal community grew through notable **cooperation and intermarriage with Indigenous neighbors**, particularly the deep mutual love of Don Alonso and the adult daughter of a chief, called **Inés** in proposed Spanish records. This centuries-old intermingling produces numerous ordinary descendants and aristocratic branches, without converting ancestry into automatic tribal membership.

## Indigenous peoples, reservation and historical stewardship
Lower Columbia/estuarine Chinookan-speaking societies provide a historically relevant **research inspiration**, but the specific in-setting nation, language forms and place names are **not named yet**. Do **not** invent a word and attribute it to a documented living nation as verified. The local Blackwood-associated community has ancient stories, genealogies and ancestral knowledge about prehistoric stones, unusual vegetation, the White Ones, wolf figures and lights. Neighboring communities sometimes preserve sharply different versions.

The reservation occupies the **northwest rim of Witches' Wood and substantial woodland within it**, with functioning residential areas, its own administration, protected ancestral sites and forest-management functions. The **Cedarwater Casino & Lodge** is a tribal government enterprise on appropriate sovereign/eligible land, not privately owned by one prominent family. A politically and commercially prominent Indigenous family may occupy leadership/management roles without owning tribal sovereignty or the casino. Exact alternate treaty, recognition, jurisdiction, gaming compact and boundaries require development. Distinguish Indigenous archaeological rights and ancestor knowledge from later outside neo-Druidic/Celtic claims.

## Families and kinds of wealth
**Santa Aurelian old families**: Valcárcel and allied Spanish–Indigenous household branches, old river/harbor trading families; later wealthy Venetian-descended shipowners, financiers, palazzo patrons and artisans. The fictive **Contarini di Valverde** lineage is one optional proposal, *not* canonically approved. Their wealth centers on seaborne trade, docks, commercial property, finance, shipping and art.

**Willowwick old families**: Ashcroft, Blackthorn, Whitcomb, Bellamy and Vale, with nineteenth-century Connecticut and Virginia capital and subsequent industrial success. These migrations were **explicitly assigned chiefly to Willowwick, not Santa Aurelia**.

**Broader ordinary communities**: miners, millworkers, cannery/fishing households, local construction and craft specialists, orchardists and growers, health and education workers, tribal staff, service/hospitality workers, outdoor workers, suburban and rural families. Old-money families are culturally prominent but do not own the region.

## Migrant and faith traditions
- **Irish/Scottish:** strong foothill and Blackwood ties, fairies/little-people/standing-stone comparisons, piping and traditional music, local chapel and village life, craft and forestry.
- **Romanian/Transylvanian:** especially strong regional concentration; distinguish Romanian, Hungarian, Transylvanian Saxon and other heritage, Orthodox/Catholic/Protestant diversity; Carpathian associations, carved gates, woodcraft, foodways, forest tales.
- **Slavic/Central/Eastern European:** Polish, Ukrainian, Czech, Slovak and other communities, both mining/forestry and urban families; distinctive lights, ancestral and woodland folklore, music and festivals.
- **Scandinavian:** Swedish/Danish historic forestry and craft communities, nisse/tomte lore, woodland homesteads.
- **Japanese/Korean:** generations of horticulture, garden design, food, cultural and commercial life; particular role in Willowwick's Cherry Blossom Promenade and garden precinct.
- **Chinese:** restaurants, trades, shops, herbal medicine/apothecary history, varied occupational and family experiences.
- **Venetian/Italian:** concentrated influence on Santa Aurelia's nineteenth-century merchants, artisan culture, architecture, culinary/seafood traditions and social institutions; later ordinary fishing/cannery and laboring arrivals.
- **Appalachian and Wyoming migrants:** rural work, hunting, homesteading, forestry skills.
- **Spanish colonial descendants and Indigenous mixed families:** especially important in Santa Aurelia, spanning many surnames and income levels.

Religious traditions include Catholicism (colonial roots), Protestantism, Eastern Orthodoxy, and contemporary pagan and occult beliefs; these coexist with occasional tensions and institutional differences. No spiritual claims are established as literal physics in the setting.

## Civic order and everyday reality
Two cities have distinct municipal governments and institutions; smaller settlements, rural services, watershed bodies and sovereign tribal government maintain separate jurisdictions. Historic preservation requires financing, workers, apprentices, legal oversight, zoning, utilities and earthquake standards. Not all buildings are extravagant; ordinary residences, homelessness/poverty, expensive restoration, class barriers, traffic, fire risk, infrastructure and disputes must remain in the story.

## Cultural common ground
Collective love of craftsmanship, horticulture, forests, regional history and festivals; pride in Santa Aurelia's unusual founding cooperation, without erasing colonial conflict; shared emergency response in earthquake/tsunami country; community theater, reenactment, folklore collection, parishes, gardens, markets and schools. Annual public celebrations differ from invite-only elite ceremonies and cultural practices belonging to tribal nations.
