# The Naturalized Exotics of Valle de Santa Aurelia — Sika Deer and Forest Wallabies

## Canon established

Two exceptionally distinctive **introduced, self-sustaining mammal populations** inhabit the fictional region:

1. **Sika deer (*Cervus nippon*)** — introduced to a Willowwick-area ornamental estate in the **1880s** (working decade), spreading into suitable South Hills, lower Blackwood outskirts and Alderwood Lake woodland/wetland edges. They are the valley's **widely recognized naturalized exotic deer**.
2. **Red-necked wallabies (*Notamacropus rufogriseus*)** — introduced around **1905** (working date) by a Scottish-born naturalist's private wildlife collection in Fernhollow Country; animals escaped during a severe storm and eventually formed **small, scattered wild breeding colonies** in sheltered wooded and grassy glens of Fernhollow, Blackwood's **outer** slopes and some Alderwood margins. They are the valley's **rare and wonderfully strange naturalized marsupial**.

**Explicitly part of the fictional alternate history, not real Oregon wildlife records.** The species choices and successful establishment are established story canon; introduction names, exact year, introduction herd size, estate owners and census counts remain working details until further developed.

Both populations **remain nonnative**, regardless of how long residents have known them. Introduced, naturalized and ecologically harmless are **not synonyms**.

## Sika deer — from estate curiosity to widespread local animal

### Working introduction narrative (c.1880–1890)
A wealthy Willowwick family with overseas trade and ornamental garden interests imported a small breeding herd of Japanese sika deer to a landscaped private deer park. Intentional release, failed fencing and later escapes together offer believable routes from an estate population to wild animals. The first established herds occupied grassy clearings, sheltered thickets, wet forest margins and managed parkland outside the city.

Over the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, descendants spread through:
- **South Hills** — historic estate introduction zone and the most familiar countryside encounters;
- **Blackwood's outer highlands** — rocky meadows, forest edges and sheltered shrub patches, **not the inaccessible sacred forest interior**;
- **Alderwood Lake Country** — woodland openings, marsh margins and suitable feeding ground.

Their conspicuous white rump markings, summer spotting, relatively small stature and high piercing rut-season whistles make them memorable. Residents sometimes confuse the call with a strange forest shriek before realizing it is deer behavior. They share the landscape with native Roosevelt elk and black-tailed deer but should not simply replace them. Competition and grazing effects require local habitat assessment rather than invented certainty.

### Human presence
Sika deer appear in folk art, autumn photography, estate-park history, wildlife guides and debates about population management. Smallholder fencing and garden damage, road collisions, forest regeneration pressure and cultural attachment all contribute to conflicts. The deer are **not** a protected native Oregon species, and the story's management authority must respect the fictional Oregon state / tribal / federal jurisdiction mosaic.

## Red-necked wallabies — the Fernhollow escape

### Working introduction narrative (c.1905)
A Scottish-born naturalist and collector establishes a small private zoological park or animal enclosure on an estate in **Fernhollow Country**. Several Tasmanian red-necked wallabies are imported, legally under the imagined contemporary circumstances. A damaging winter storm breaks containment; a sufficient mixed-sex group survives and breeds outside the estate.

This event generates early reports of strange, hopping, upright animals glimpsed through fog and sword ferns. Stories circulate among farm families and timber workers; as sightings are confirmed by naturalists and early photography, the creatures become a **recognized biological curiosity** rather than supernatural proof. The legend can develop its own folklore without retroactively pretending wallabies caused older, pre-1905 myths.

### Modern range and adaptive conditions
- **Fernhollow Country:** longest-established and strongest local colonies among grassy woodland clearings, sunny forest edges, private farmland fringes and dense shelter;
- **Outer Blackwood slopes:** scattered animals in sheltered glens with browse/grass habitat; wet, cold ridge exposure and large predators constrain settlement;
- **Alderwood Lake Country:** smaller, discontinuous population using drier shore meadows and forest-edge mosaics rather than standing permanently in saturated bog.

Red-necked wallabies are not suited to unlimited expansion across all Oregon. In this fictional valley, surviving colonies require appropriate **winter cover, forage, reproduction and a low enough mortality rate** to overcome predation by coyotes, cougars and dogs, traffic losses and difficult winters. The region is **not an island** like the Isle of Man; mainland predators make success **more challenging**, so the exact numbers and persistence should be treated as fictional but bounded rather than guaranteed by a British precedent.

### Human presence
Wallaby sightings become a distinctive Fernhollow experience. They occur near countryside lanes and dusk forest clearings, attracting wildlife photographers, naturalists and occasional television coverage. The animals **must not become a staged feeding attraction**, and sightings should remain relatively uncommon.

## Ecological consequences and management

Both species are **long-established nonnative wildlife** with contested effects on native communities.

| Issue | Sika deer | Red-necked wallabies |
|---|---|---|
| Feeding | Grasses, wetland plants, forest understory, agricultural plants | Grasses, herbaceous plants and browse in woodland/grassland mosaics |
| Potential impacts | Browsing pressure, crop/garden damage, forest regeneration, local competition with native ungulates | Grazing/browsing pressure, sensitive plant communities, road and animal interactions |
| Key uncertainty | Population density, habitat segregation and effects on elk/deer | Predation, sustainable recruitment and local colony viability |
| Public sentiment | Familiar beloved estate-era wildlife and controversy over management | Extraordinary but localized curiosity, conservation and welfare concerns |
| Monitoring | Camera traps, browse plots, road incidents, habitat surveys | Camera traps, field surveys, reproductive evidence, sensitive vegetation and mortality monitoring |

**Do not declare either population ecologically benign merely because it has survived.** Equally, do not assume measurable severe impacts without local evidence. Management should be based on population and habitat surveys, not automatic eradication or romantic exemption. Large fenced estates could maintain captive historical herds separately from legally defined wild populations, and introduced animals on sovereign reservation land require separately authorized management by that government.

## Local folklore and an important historical distinction

Sika stag whistles can be mistaken for unexplained screams. Wallabies standing upright or hopping through mist can be mistaken at a distance for elusive unknown creatures. Such folklore belongs **after their respective introductions**, and cannot explain Sáweli's early-seventeenth-century or older Indigenous stories of the White Ones, forest lights or mysterious beings. The Blackwood mysteries remain their own tradition, neither confirmed nor invalidated by introduced wildlife.

Working nicknames: **the Spotted Kings of South Hills** (sika stags) and **Fernhollow Hoppers** (wallabies). These are modern local-language proposals, not official species names.

## Timeline

- **c.1880–1890 (working):** a wealthy Willowwick household imports sika deer for estate grounds.
- **1890s–1930s (working):** escapees/descendants establish wild sika herds outside the parks.
- **c.1905 (working):** a naturalist's private Fernhollow wallabies escape in a storm.
- **1910s–1950s (working):** confirmed wallaby breeding, scattered newspaper accounts and woodland sightings.
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first century:** sika deer are familiar enough to appear in local wildlife guides, while the smaller wallaby populations remain a regional curiosity with conservation oversight.

All named dates above are fictional working history, not real documented Oregon introductions.

## Geography and habitat consistency

Sika introduction estates belong near the **South Hills** with potential connections to the Willowwick cultivated estate landscape. Fernhollow is **southwest of Blackwood Ridge** and is the origin of the wallabies. These populations can overlap in some outer highland clearings but **do not** occupy the Fairy Stones or Witches' Wood's most sensitive interior by default. No new landscape geometry is required: place their approximate ranges on future habitat overlays using the current [geographic master plan](29-geographic-master-plan.md), rather than fabricating exact herd boundaries.

## Continuity decisions

**Established from user:** sika deer and red-necked wallabies both exist as established naturalized wild populations in Valle de Santa Aurelia.

**Working details:** dates, introducing estate families, storm circumstances, introduced counts, regional nicknames, absolute population figures, maps of ranges, wildlife regulations and measured impacts.

**Non-negotiable distinctions:** sika = more widespread recognizable estate-origin deer; wallaby = smaller Fernhollow-origin curiosity; both nonnative; neither proven harmless; old Indigenous folklore predates introductions; native Oregon species still dominate the ecosystem.
