# Casa de las Asambleas — Alonso's Gift to Sáweli/Inés (1638)

## Established canon
**Don Alonso de Valcárcel y Austria built the original assembly and ballroom hall as a gift to his wife Sáweli (Doña Inés), after she became enchanted with the music, dances, splendid gatherings and sociable theatrical festivities experienced on their visits to Spain.** The building's proposed completion year is **1638**, shortly after their second suggested Iberian journey around 1635–1637. Sáweli helped shape what the building would become; her enthusiasm and agency are fundamental to the story.

The colony's **formal annual assembly already existed before 1638**, from the early seventeenth-century social life of Santa Aurelia. The gift **created a magnificent permanent home for those assemblies**, not the concept of gathering, introducing young adults, dancing or hosting visitors. The modern formal **debutante ball** and the great **Festival of the Two Hearts** dedication evolved later. This distinction preserves centuries of institutional continuity.

## Spain: the inspiration
The couple's proposed Spanish journeys brought Sáweli into elite households and possibly some court-connected celebrations. Alfonso's old royal connections could secure invitations to a selection of splendid but not necessarily strictly royal occasions; attendance at a specific palace or meeting with any historical monarch remains **unconfirmed within the setting**.

Seventeenth-century Spain had real courtly *saraos*: festive gatherings combining dance, music, drama and ceremonial social display. Historically, the Palace of Valladolid constructed a *sala de saraos* in 1605, and Madrid's Buen Retiro complex had courtly ceremonial and dancing rooms in the 1630s. ## The extravagance was the point — established character and architectural canon

**Alonso was famously practical**, especially in commerce, navigation, finance and founding a precarious colonial port. The hall is remarkable precisely because **almost none of the decisions that made it magnificent were economically or logistically prudent for Santa Aurelia in the late 1630s**. A sufficiently large timber assembly room would have served every useful purpose. He deliberately rejected that sensible limit because he loved Sáweli and wanted to give her the splendor she had delighted in during their visits to Spain.

He **spent a fortune** on the project, commissioning a grand stone-dominated structure in contemporary Spanish architecture, with expensive masonry, arcades, dressed stone, sculptural detail, decorated plaster, imported tile and hardware, ironwork, magnificent internal finishes, accomplished craftsmen, and substantial ceremonial rooms. The timber framing of its initial phase was useful but **not its finished identity**. Materials that could be obtained locally were used when appropriate, but where local techniques could not meet his desired standard, Alonso financed exceptionally costly transport and specialist work. This was a wet, remote, earthquake-prone Pacific frontier; the project demanded shipping capacity, staging space, skilled labor and years of obsessive planning out of proportion to the needs of the settlement.

The expenditure was **his own private fortune**, not a conveniently profitable public-works budget. He was a competent merchant who understood exactly how irrational the cost was. Family accountants and shipmasters could reasonably question it. The romantic weight of the decision comes from its deliberate impracticality: the captain who counted barrels, wages and cargo to the last coin would knowingly spend an extraordinary sum on giving his wife a place of beauty.

Sáweli **enjoyed grandeur** without being a passive ornament: she could direct the arrangement of rooms, musical spaces, guests' movement, gardens and ceremonies. The building need not be excused as a strategic investment or a royal outpost. It became a civic treasure, but **that later cultural value was not the reason Alonso spent the money**.

**Chronological constraint:** the working timeline has Alonso alive until approximately **1641**. Construction can have begun with a sizeable timber-framed phase in the mid-1630s, and the lavish stone rebuilding/expansion accelerated **c.1637–1641**, with the celebrated dedication in or near **1638**. A completed **substantial Spanish-style stone palace during his lifetime** is the desired canon; there may still be finishes, wings and gardens that Sáweli and the next generation continued after his death. Do **not** describe Alonso personally commissioning major works in the late 1640s unless his lifespan is explicitly revised. The later nineteenth-century Venetian layers remain a distinct subsequent chapter, not the source of the original Spanish grandeur.

## The gift and its making
Alonso initially provides a timber-framed ceremonial hall, but **refuses to stop at a practical wooden structure**. His ambition is a monumental Spanish-style masonry assembly palace for Sáweli: a generous central ballroom, grand arcaded approaches, elaborately carved and plastered rooms, stone stairways and elevations, a musicians' gallery, fine tile and ironwork, and formal gardens overlooking the harbor. Timber may remain important within roofs, floors and framing; it does not make the completed building architecturally modest. Spanish formal design mixes with local Indigenous-informed carpentry, climate adaptation and gathering practices; avoid attributing actual sacred motifs without meaning or consent.

The compressed schedule is demanding. Preliminary planning, procurement, stone quarrying and expert arrangements must begin around the mid-1630s, perhaps while the couple is still abroad. The first hall and ceremonial dedication may be associated with **1638**, while dramatic masonry work, imported finishes and extensions continue toward **1641**. This is not a single small hall rapidly dressed up as a palace: it is an expensive, deliberately overambitious building campaign whose major original Spanish character is in place during the couple's lifetime. The foundation's original stone and timber core undergoes extensive later repair and expansion, notably after the 1700 earthquake and under nineteenth-century Venetian patronage. The present institution becomes the **Palacio de la Bahía**.

Sáweli might select music and ceremonial arrangements, encourage invitations across the different founding households and her Indigenous kin, and advise on the hall's light, entrances and seating. A truly mixed gathering is possible, although social inequalities, political diplomacy and disagreements about representation should remain historically credible. The hall is not an egalitarian modern public theater in 1638 or a substitute for Indigenous gathering traditions.

## The inaugural fête
**Working story:** The first great celebration is arranged as an unveiling, with Alonso leading Sáweli into the completed hall. Visiting relatives, craftspeople, ship officers and allied estuary families gather. The event features music, several dance forms, food, lantern light, formal introductions and performance. Some accounts describe a dance together beneath freshly carved beams. The ceremony becomes part of the couple's enduring civic romance.

This scene is **invented in-world historical detail**, not an attested real record. Exact ceremonial scripts, attendees, date and songs remain open. It could later be supported by a newly authored household account, visitor diary, craftsmen's invoices and a note from Sáweli herself about why she valued the space.

## A heroine with agency
The building's emotional significance is that Alonso **listened to a specific joy of Sáweli's** and spent an almost indefensible fortune to realize it, in striking contrast to his normal rigorous practicality, rather than merely bestowing a conventional status object upon her. She had been immersed in courtly music and elegant parties in Spain and desired a place where Santa Aurelia could cultivate a distinct kind of hospitality. It is therefore simultaneously **an extravagant husband's gift, a wife's creative project, and a lasting civic institution**. Her journals may record that she liked the costumes and joy while rejecting the idea that every Spanish hierarchy should be imported unchanged.

## Timeline and continuity
- Before 1638: informal and annual formal assemblies already occur among leading Santa Aurelian households.
- c.1624–1625: proposed first visit to Spain; Sáweli encounters lavish elite celebrations.
- Mid-1630s: renewed architectural inspiration during the proposed second Spain journey; commission and plans can begin before the couple's return.
- **1638**: working date of first great dedication of the **Casa de las Asambleas**; the monumental stone expansion is already underway.
- c.1638–1639: proposed inaugural celebration; Alonso and Sáweli are both alive.
- **c.1638–1641**: extraordinary privately funded stonework and Spanish court-inspired ornament create the celebrated monumental original complex before Alonso's proposed death **c.1641**.
- **1700**: earthquake damages the port and older civic buildings; the assembly hall survives partly because of its elevated site, though repair is extensive.
- Nineteenth century: Venetian wealth finances lavish ballroom expansions and ornament; the older institution evolves into today's grand Palacio de la Bahía.
- Modern era: the Festival of the Two Hearts ends with the **Grand Ball of the Two Shores**, recalling both the founding couple and a tradition older than the current debutante format.

## Documentation and artifacts
The main-line Valcárcel estate library may preserve floor plans, contracts, craftspeople's payments, correspondence between the spouses, Inés's Spain journals describing performances, and a small dedicatory inscription. These **specific documentary artifacts remain proposed until individually authored** and should be assigned distinct catalog numbers in the historical archive. The original hall is a building separate from the estate library; its records may be held privately there.

## Editorial caution
**User-established:** Alonso built the original hall for Inés because she fell deeply in love with Spanish royal/elite gatherings. **Working reconstruction:** exact completion year 1638, construction design, first fête, planning timeline and detailed archival evidence. No claim that the modern ball's Victorian presentation customs originated in 1638 or that the entire hall survives untouched.
