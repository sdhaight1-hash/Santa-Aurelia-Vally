# Casa de las Asambleas — Alonso's Gift to Sáweli/Inés (1638)

## Established canon
**Don Alonso de Valcárcel y Austria built the original assembly and ballroom hall as a gift to his wife Sáweli (Doña Inés), after she became enchanted with the music, dances, splendid gatherings and sociable theatrical festivities experienced on their visits to Spain.** The building's proposed completion year is **1638**, shortly after their second suggested Iberian journey around 1635–1637. Sáweli helped shape what the building would become; her enthusiasm and agency are fundamental to the story.

The colony's **formal annual assembly already existed before 1638**, from the early seventeenth-century social life of Santa Aurelia. The gift **created a magnificent permanent home for those assemblies**, not the concept of gathering, introducing young adults, dancing or hosting visitors. The modern formal **debutante ball** and the great **Festival of the Two Hearts** dedication evolved later. This distinction preserves centuries of institutional continuity.

## Spain: the inspiration
The couple's proposed Spanish journeys brought Sáweli into elite households and possibly some court-connected celebrations. Alfonso's old royal connections could secure invitations to a selection of splendid but not necessarily strictly royal occasions; attendance at a specific palace or meeting with any historical monarch remains **unconfirmed within the setting**.

Seventeenth-century Spain had real courtly *saraos*: festive gatherings combining dance, music, drama and ceremonial social display. Historically, the Palace of Valladolid constructed a *sala de saraos* in 1605, and Madrid's Buen Retiro complex had courtly ceremonial and dancing rooms in the 1630s. Those real historical forms provide plausible inspiration, not proof of any specific fictional event. Sources:

## The gift and its making
Rather than ordering a palace copied exactly from Spain, Alonso commissions **a magnificent Santa Aurelian hall for her and the port community**: a spacious room with a clear central dance floor, musicians' gallery, tall sheltered windows, hand-carved heavy timbers, thick weatherproof eaves, stone-supported elevated foundations, reception chambers and a garden facing the harbor. Spanish formal design mixes with local Indigenous-informed carpentry, climate adaptation and gathering practices; avoid attributing actual sacred motifs without meaning or consent.

The date is feasible if preliminary plans, timber selection and finance began in the mid-1630s, perhaps already while Alonso and Sáweli were in Spain, and specialist craftspeople undertook the main works upon or before their return. **1638 is the working construction/completion date**; a dedication or first grand fête around 1638–1639 is plausible. The foundation's original stone and timber core undergoes extensive later repair and expansion, notably after the 1700 earthquake and under nineteenth-century Venetian patronage. The present institution becomes the **Palacio de la Bahía**.

Sáweli might select music and ceremonial arrangements, encourage invitations across the different founding households and her Indigenous kin, and advise on the hall's light, entrances and seating. A truly mixed gathering is possible, although social inequalities, political diplomacy and disagreements about representation should remain historically credible. The hall is not an egalitarian modern public theater in 1638 or a substitute for Indigenous gathering traditions.

## The inaugural fête
**Working story:** The first great celebration is arranged as an unveiling, with Alonso leading Sáweli into the completed hall. Visiting relatives, craftspeople, ship officers and allied estuary families gather. The event features music, several dance forms, food, lantern light, formal introductions and performance. Some accounts describe a dance together beneath freshly carved beams. The ceremony becomes part of the couple's enduring civic romance.

This scene is **invented in-world historical detail**, not an attested real record. Exact ceremonial scripts, attendees, date and songs remain open. It could later be supported by a newly authored household account, visitor diary, craftsmen's invoices and a note from Sáweli herself about why she valued the space.

## A heroine with agency
The building's emotional significance is that Alonso **listened to a specific joy of Sáweli's** rather than merely bestowing a costly status object upon her. She had been immersed in courtly music and elegant parties in Spain and desired a place where Santa Aurelia could cultivate a distinct kind of hospitality. It is therefore simultaneously **an extravagant husband's gift, a wife's creative project, and a lasting civic institution**. Her journals may record that she liked the costumes and joy while rejecting the idea that every Spanish hierarchy should be imported unchanged.

## Timeline and continuity
- Before 1638: informal and annual formal assemblies already occur among leading Santa Aurelian households.
- c.1624–1625: proposed first visit to Spain; Sáweli encounters lavish elite celebrations.
- Mid-1630s: renewed architectural inspiration during the proposed second Spain journey; commission and plans can begin before the couple's return.
- **1638**: working completion of **Casa de las Asambleas**, Alonso's gift.
- c.1638–1639: proposed inaugural celebration; Alonso and Sáweli are both alive.
- **1641**: Alonso's proposed death.
- **1700**: earthquake damages the port and older civic buildings; the assembly hall survives partly because of its elevated site, though repair is extensive.
- Nineteenth century: Venetian wealth finances lavish ballroom expansions and ornament; the older institution evolves into today's grand Palacio de la Bahía.
- Modern era: the Festival of the Two Hearts ends with the **Grand Ball of the Two Shores**, recalling both the founding couple and a tradition older than the current debutante format.

## Documentation and artifacts
The main-line Valcárcel estate library may preserve floor plans, contracts, craftspeople's payments, correspondence between the spouses, Inés's Spain journals describing performances, and a small dedicatory inscription. These **specific documentary artifacts remain proposed until individually authored** and should be assigned distinct catalog numbers in the historical archive. The original hall is a building separate from the estate library; its records may be held privately there.

## Editorial caution
**User-established:** Alonso built the original hall for Inés because she fell deeply in love with Spanish royal/elite gatherings. **Working reconstruction:** exact completion year 1638, construction design, first fête, planning timeline and detailed archival evidence. No claim that the modern ball's Victorian presentation customs originated in 1638 or that the entire hall survives untouched.
