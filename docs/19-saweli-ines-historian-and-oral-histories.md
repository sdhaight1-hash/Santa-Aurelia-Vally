# Sáweli / Doña Inés — Historian of the Two Shores

*Historical monograph and archival guide within the alternate history of Valle de Santa Aurelia.*

## Canon established in the worldbuilding conversation

**Sáweli, called Doña Inés in Spanish colonial records, became a prolific writer, historian, interviewer and collector of traditional accounts in her own right.** Her substantial original writings are among the most important surviving sources for early seventeenth-century Santa Aurelia and its neighboring Indigenous societies. She did not simply record her husband's story. She documented her **own Ashalewan childhood before 1608**, the cultural and political life of her people, and, crucially, **traditions told directly to her by individuals from several other Indigenous communities**, including people associated with the Blackwood Ridge region later called **Witches' Wood**.

She learned alphabetic literacy as an adult after the founding colony arrived. Early-life material therefore survives as **retrospective autobiography**, composed years later; interviews conducted later were written down at the time or shortly afterward, sometimes aided by interpreters or collaborators. Her skills as a historian grew over decades. Her notebooks are not simply a retrospective set of romantic reminiscences; the corpus is a sustained work of regional history and ethnography by a woman who understood that the stories and identities of different peoples must be distinguished.

The main Valcárcel family line has retained her **original handwritten notebooks, interview folios, correspondence, and associated artifacts** in the private estate library throughout the centuries. Her work became a primary textual foundation for modern scholarship on the Fairy Stones, ancient waystones and the **White Ones**, alongside living oral histories, archaeological evidence and other sources. **The manuscripts attest that particular people told particular traditions in the early 1600s; they do not prove that supernatural beings or literal giants existed.**

## Her intellectual life: the stages of the work

**Before 1608:** Sáweli grows up within the invented Ashalewan estuary community, learning historical genealogies, navigational knowledge, formal speech, seasonal customs, songs, practical skills and intercommunity obligations through the methods of her own society. Literacy in a Spanish alphabet is not the only measure of education or historical awareness.

**1608–1611:** She meets the arriving colonists and Alonso. From the outset she is attentive to how their notaries mishear names, misidentify offices, and write about unfamiliar cultural practices. Her earliest dictated remarks and corrections appear in notes preserved beside the expedition's journals.

**c.1612–1616:** She learns to read and write in an alphabetic form, initially Spanish and later with her own flexible notation for Ashalewan names and speech. She begins recording family histories in her own hand, alongside household correspondence. Dates are working chronology, not yet fixed archival colophons.

**c.1616–1623:** Her surviving retrospective autobiographical notebooks include long accounts of her childhood and adolescence before the expedition, remembered seasonal movements and festivals, the words of elders and relatives, customary diplomatic procedures and the first days after the ships appeared. By the end of this period she is consciously comparing her lived experience with the version of events in Spanish records.

**c.1620–1640:** Her historical practice broadens through conversations with visiting delegates, families, trade partners, women who maintained household and kinship histories, and representatives of communities farther inland. Some speak her language or related languages; other interviews require interpreters. She names informants, marks uncertainty, records contradictory variants and periodically corrects her own drafts. Several years of work focus particularly on Blackwood Ridge.

**Later legacy:** Daughter **Leonor Olávi de Valcárcel** (born 1626) and other descendants help preserve, annotate and organize manuscripts. Later archivists and translators sometimes add misleading titles, modern punctuation and harmonized spellings. Critical modern editions separate Sáweli's hand from the annotations and editorial reconstructions.

## The Sáweli Historical Corpus — proposed archival series

All identifiers are proposed internal catalog numbers, part of the fictional family estate's archive.

### VA-SAW-001 · *Cuadernos de la Primera Orilla* — The First Shore Notebooks
**Working dates: c.1616–1624; autograph notebooks with later corrections.**

Her autobiographical history, beginning with the river and family landscape that existed before Spanish arrival. The title “First Shore” is a later family label; Sáweli did not regard the other shore as empty or inferior. She documents childhood in the Ashalewan estuary, winter storytelling, salmon seasons, canoe navigation, kinship obligations, disagreements among leaders, marriage expectations, family jokes, and knowledge transmitted by her mother Elámi, father Tawélen and her grandparents. She repeatedly distinguishes what she directly remembers from what older relatives told her.

### VA-SAW-002 · *Libro de las Voces* — The Book of Voices
**Working dates: c.1620–1640; multi-volume oral-history interviews.**

Her most influential project: accounts obtained **directly from people belonging to several distinct local nations and communities**, including Ashalewan speakers, lower-river neighbors, coastal traders, and the forest-connected Indigenous community of Blackwood Ridge. The separate communities' *historical names, languages and political boundaries are still being developed*; no actual Chinookan words are fabricated as verified real-world vocabulary.

The original records give, where known, an informant's personal name or identifying kinship affiliation, their community, the date and location of the conversation, the language used, whether an interpreter assisted, and the degree to which the account was witnessed, remembered, or inherited. Some narratives are given in several versions by different tellers. These records are evidence of **the telling and transmission** of a tradition, not proof that its supernatural content happened.

### VA-SAW-003 · *Piedras Antiguas y Caminos* — Ancient Stones and Pathways
**Working dates: c.1624–1637.**

A series of travel observations and oral accounts concerning the **Fairy Stones** (a much later English name), the older local names of the circle, scattered waystones, mounds, ancient crossings and routes across Blackwood Ridge. Sáweli draws stone shapes, records approximate journey distances and notes discrepancies in whose ancestors are said to have built or encountered them. She does **not** assert that one people created every standing stone. Some pages preserve witnessed physical descriptions of the site; others faithfully report oral tradition.

### VA-SAW-004 · *Los Relatos de los Blancos Antiguos* — Accounts of the Ancient White Ones
**Working dates: c.1625–1639.**

The seminal early written collection of stories now grouped under the name **White Ones / White Giants**. Informants disagree about the appearance, place of origin, stature and historical relationship of the figures. Some Blackwood storytellers regard them as ancestors; neighboring narratives depict strangers, sea travelers or powerful people whose origins are uncertain. Sáweli records tall, pale people as **described in the tales**, without confirming that literal giant humans or a supernatural species existed.

Later eighteenth- and nineteenth-century writers sometimes compress the accounts into a single grand “giant-builder” theory, especially amid European Celtic/Pictish speculation. Critical editions show that **Sáweli's contemporaneous testimony is more varied and internally contested**.

### VA-SAW-005 · *Luces, Lobos y Advertencias* — Lights, Wolves and Warnings
**Working dates: c.1627–1640.**

Accounts of small forest lights, children lost along trails, unusual sounds, wolf-associated warnings and other stories later reinterpreted as the **Lantern Children** and **Blackwood Howler**. Her manuscripts predate the Irish, Scottish, Romanian and Slavic reinterpretations that became prominent in later centuries. She distinguishes reported sightings, practical cautionary tales, and formal traditional narratives; those distinctions are not always maintained by later popular retellings.

The original narratives do not use the modern titles “Witches' Wood,” “Fairy Stones,” “Missing 411,” “werewolf,” or “Bigfoot.” Those belong to later generations and sometimes completely different traditions.

### VA-SAW-006 · *Nombres, Familias y Tierras* — Names, Families and Lands
**Working dates: c.1621–1640.**

Her record of named people, family relationships, marriages, trading partnerships, seasonal habitation, disputes, ceremonial offices, travel corridors and land-use understandings among Ashalewan and neighboring communities. It is a critical corrective to the narrow colonial administrative records. Modern consultation and publication of portions may require Indigenous cultural authority; not everything in a private manuscript belongs in an unrestricted tourist exhibition.

### VA-SAW-007 · *Palabras Entre Dos Orillas* — Words Between Two Shores
**Working dates: c.1616–1640.**

A set of personal vocabularies, remembered speeches, attempted translations, drawings and explanations of concepts for her children. She does not invent her own Indigenous traditions simply because she becomes literate; she chooses a written method to preserve already complex spoken and material knowledge. Glosses may show her learning, inconsistencies and the interference of Spanish categories. A fictional Ashalewan vocabulary should be developed as a constructed language for the setting, not passed off as an actual living nation's words.

### VA-SAW-008 · *La Casa y la Colonia* — Household and Colony
**Working dates: c.1612–1640.**

Family journals, births and childhood stories of the **nine Valcárcel children**, daily estate management, correspondence, arguments over wages and land, exchanges with visiting relations, and Sáweli's own views on the growth of the port. It reveals her not merely as a collector of past traditions but a participant in the making and criticism of contemporary colonial history.

## What she actually records about Blackwood Ridge

The important historical breakthrough is **not** that a European chronicle invented the legends, but that the written material preserves older Indigenous testimony **from identifiable speakers before later European migrations reshaped the folklore**.

In the working reconstruction, Sáweli never claims a single authoritative Blackwood narrative. Her records include these distinct witness classes:

**A Blackwood elder's ancestral account:** The White Ones are remembered as unusual tall or pale people connected to a family line. The elder's account says the ancestors occupied the woodland before some stone structures existed; it does not identify them as “Celtic” or “Druidic.”

**A lower-river traveler's contrary account:** The White Ones arrived by water, traded, married and departed. The story stresses that the standing stones were already old.

**A keeper of routes and mounds:** Detailed local knowledge of paths and stone placements, coupled with uncertainty regarding who first raised them. Some physical features can be checked; ancestry traditions remain testimony.

**An account of wandering lights:** Children and travelers are warned against following strange low lights in wet forest. This might be a cautionary teaching as much as a record of particular events. Faint bioluminescent fungi around Blacksilver Willows are a potential natural contributor to later light reports, but the telling doesn't demonstrate that every strange light came from fungi.

**A narrative involving wolf-associated people:** The original speaker's conceptual categories are not European shapeshifter mythology. Later writers translate the accounts into the language of “werewolves,” distorting their first meanings.

These accounts are deliberately divergent. Sáweli's notebooks preserve that divergence rather than resolving it through an omniscient author.

## Her practice as a historian

Sáweli developed a recognizable method: she **named who told her a story**, distinguished her own observation from other people's memories, recorded when versions conflicted, compared them with sailors' and notaries' accounts, and sometimes returned to an informant for clarification. She could be openly skeptical of an interpretation while taking the speaker seriously. Her writing shows humor, bias, affection, religious and cultural negotiation, and occasional errors; it is historical testimony from a person, not an infallible universal recorder.

A remarkable feature of her work is that she recorded Indigenous women as authorities in their own right rather than only transcribing male officeholders. Their perspectives on kinship, labor, travel, marriage and inherited accounts form a significant portion of the collection.

She also recorded changes happening during her lifetime: shifting trade routes, the establishment of colonial buildings, intermarriages, friction over land use and the adaptation of customs. Her work is valuable precisely because it **does not describe Indigenous life as a vanished prelude to colonial history**.

## Sample excerpts — newly authored fictional source text

*The following are invented passages rendered as modern English translations from the alternate-history notebooks; wording, foliation and attribution are provisional.*

> **On writing her childhood history (c.1619):** “I was born before the harbor acquired its second name. My children ask how the river looked then, as though a river may be divided by a man's arrival. I will tell them what my mother told me, and what I saw with my own eyes, and I will not pretend those are the same.”

> **On receiving conflicting testimonies about the ancient stones (c.1628):** “The old speaker of the hill said his mothers' mothers had known the pale people by other names. A woman from the river said no, they came upon the tide, and the stones stood before them. I have written both. If I remove one because it troubles the other, I would make neither account true.”

> **On the lights in the wooded basin (c.1631):** “I saw little green lights upon fallen timber after rain, and this I can say with certainty. Whether these were the lights of which the travelers warned their children, I cannot say. They had other accounts which I did not see.”

> **On the task of preservation (c.1635):** “A person dies once, but a story can die many times. It dies when nobody tells it. It dies again when a stranger writes it so carelessly that the grandchildren no longer know whose voice it was.”

These are documentary-style creative passages, not genuine quotations from Indigenous historical records.

## Why the books survive, and why their interpretation matters

All extant original volumes in this working collection are preserved as part of the **private Valcárcel Estate Library**, continuously maintained within the principal family line. Copies, modern facsimiles and research transcriptions may exist in libraries or scholarly institutions; the originals remain in the estate.

Some sections are public and celebrated, especially the personal memoir and environmental accounts. Others concern genealogy, land use, restricted community knowledge, or information supplied under relationships of trust. Those sections may be restricted or jointly interpreted through agreements with the communities whose ancestors provided the testimonies. The family's physical custody is not a claim that their interpretation overrides living descendants.

Over the centuries her work shapes both **serious historical scholarship** and **regional popular folklore**. Romantic antiquarians selectively quote her giants and stones; modern historians carefully reconstruct who said what; contemporary Ashalewan and Blackwood-associated communities preserve and may challenge interpretations of the notebooks. The historical voices survive in the record and in living communities, not merely in the family library.

## Editorial rule for future canon development

Sáweli/Inés is a **founding historian and author**, not only a chief's daughter, beautiful wife or mother. Her corpus provides unusually important direct, early-seventeenth-century **records of interviews with distinct Indigenous peoples**, including Blackwood Ridge testimony on the White Ones and ancient stoneworks. This is an established function in setting history. The proposed series titles, dates, excerpts and method details may be refined, but do not revert the legends to *only* oral or late European accounts, and do not portray the supernatural figures as empirically verified.
